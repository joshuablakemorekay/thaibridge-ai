Preface

I noticed that people, myself included, as English speakers, were frustrated during morning and evening chanting with the Thai monks, since they more often than not read from a Thai-script-only chanting book. So anyone who was not Thai could perhaps feel left out, because the fact is that most people don't speak or understand Thai well enough, let alone read it! Nor do they care to learn it, because frankly the English language is universal and powerful enough.

So I decided to use my AI skills and tools on my laptop to create a digital publication, a digital chanting book, as a feature in my ThaiBridge AI language, culture and Buddhism learning web app, accessible to all. The only way to be international is to speak English. English is a very powerful language, and the other translations are relevant too, making the book even more accessible and interesting. I value being wholesome and inclusive.

A guide to pronunciation

Each verse in this book can be read in two romanised forms: the Pali in the Latin alphabet, and the Thai translation in ThaiBridge Spelling. This guide covers both.

Pali

Pali is the language in which the Theravāda teachings were recorded and preserved. It is what is chanted. Most letters sound as you would expect; these are the ones to know.

Vowels

ShortAs inLongAs in
aaboutāfather
ihitīmachine
uputūrule
egrey
ogo

A long vowel is held about twice as long as a short one. e and o are short before a double consonant: mettā, sotthi.

Consonants

  • c is ch as in church, never k or s.
  • ñ is ny as in canyon.
  • ṅ is ng as in sing; ṃ is a nasal hum at the end of a syllable, said ng in Thai temples.
  • v is softer than English v, close to w.
  • g is always hard, as in go; s always as in sun.
  • kh, gh, ch, jh, ṭh, ḍh, th, dh, ph, bh are single sounds with a puff of breath. th is never as in the, and ph is never f.
  • ṭ, ṭh, ḍ, ḍh, ṇ, ḷ are said with the tip of the tongue curled back.
  • A double consonant is said twice, or held: bud-dha, dham-ma.

Pali as it is chanted in a Thai temple

The line in Thai script is what is chanted, and Thai monks chant Pali with Thai sounds. What you hear will often differ from the romanised line, which shows the classical spelling. The commonest differences:

  • A final ṃ is said ng: buddhaṃ is chanted put-tang.
  • bh, dh, gh are said p, t, k with breath: dhammaṃ is tam-mang.
  • v is said w; the curled-back letters are said like plain t, d, n, l.

When in doubt, follow the Thai-script line and the voices around you.

ThaiBridge Spelling

The Thai translations are given in ThaiBridge Spelling, the system ThaiBridge AI uses to teach Thai. It is based on Paiboon+ by Benjawan Poomsan Becker (Paiboon Publishing).

Vowels

A doubled letter is a long vowel: a / aa, i / ii, u / uu, e / ee, o / oo. Four letters are not in the English alphabet:

LetterSoundExample
ɔas in lawขอ kɔ̌ɔ, to ask
ɛas in bed, held openแม่ mɛ̂ɛ, mother
əas in her, with no rเดิน dəən, to walk
ʉoo with the lips spread, not roundedมือ mʉʉ, hand

ia, ua and ʉa glide from one vowel into the next.

Consonants

  • k, p, t, ch are breathy, as in kit, pit, tip, chip.
  • g, bp, dt, j have no breath: g like the k in skin, bp like the p in spin, dt like the t in stop.
  • ŋ is ng as in sing, and can begin a syllable: ŋaan, work.
  • A final k, t, p is stopped, not released.

Endings in i and o (or y and w)

In this book a final ย is written i (dûai, nɔ́ɔi, ləəi) and a final ว is written o (lɛ́ɛo, kǎao). You will also see these endings written y and w: the ThaiBridge AI app writes dûay and nɔ́ɔy, and some systems write lɛ́ɛw. They are the same sounds, a short glide to i or to o at the end of the vowel.

Tones

Thai has five tones, and the mark over the vowel shows which. Said with a different tone, the same syllable is a different word.

MarkToneExample
kaamid (no mark): levelคา, to be stuck
kàalow: level, below your normal voiceข่า, galangal
kâafalling: high, droppingค่า, value
káahigh: raisedค้า, to trade
kǎarising: low, then upขา, leg

A hyphen joins the syllables of one word: sà-rá-ná, refuge.

Chants for each day of the week

In Thai tradition each day of the week has its own number, the power of the day (กำลังวัน, kamlang wan), and its own protection chant. The chant of the day you were born on is recited that number of times. The numbers come from Thai astrology and are a custom, not a requirement of the Buddha's teaching. Every chant below is already in this book: each is a protection chant or its opening verse.
