🌏 Practising the Dhamma Anywhere ปฏิบัติธรรมได้ทุกที่

The teaching does not arrive with a country attached to it. This page is about taking it up where you already are.

You may have arrived here wondering whether Buddhism asks you to take on somebody else's customs — a language, a temple, a set of manners that are not yours. It does not. What follows is the practice itself, in the form the Buddha gave it to lay people, and you will notice that it names no country, no nationality and no ceremony.

It names no country on purpose. Not because culture does not matter, but because none of this depends on which one you live in. How Thai culture fits alongside it →

Where it starts: the Five Precepts

Five undertakings a person makes for themselves. They are training rules, not commandments — you take them up, and when you fall short you take them up again. No temple, no ceremony and no permission from anyone is required to begin.

  1. To refrain from taking life. pāṇātipātā veramaṇī
  2. To refrain from taking what is not given. adinnādānā veramaṇī
  3. To refrain from sexual misconduct. kāmesu micchācārā veramaṇī
  4. To refrain from false speech. musāvādā veramaṇī
  5. To refrain from intoxicants that cloud the mind. surāmeraya-majja-pamādaṭṭhānā veramaṇī

The five training rules (pañca-sīla) undertaken by lay Buddhists throughout the Theravada world — and, in the same words, everywhere else.

A complete practice, in three parts

The Buddha described three grounds for making merit — puñña-kiriya-vatthu. Between them they cover a whole life of practice, and not one of them requires an institution, a building or a language you do not speak.

Giving

dāna

Generosity of things, of time, of attention, of patience. It is measured by the state of mind behind it, not by the size of what is given.

Virtue

sīla

The precepts above, lived out in ordinary conduct — what you do, what you say, and how you treat whoever is in front of you.

Cultivation

bhāvanā

Training the mind: meditation, mindfulness, and the steady development of understanding. Meditation guidance →

The Buddha's own map for lay life

In the Sigālovāda Sutta, the Buddha meets a young householder performing an inherited ritual of bowing to the six directions, and gives the practice a different meaning: each direction stands for a set of relationships, and honouring them properly is the practice. It has been called the layperson's code of discipline ever since.

It is worth noticing what this list is made of. Every society that has ever existed has these relationships in some form. That is why the teaching travels without needing to be translated into anyone's customs — the customs differ, the relationships do not.

Parents and children

The east

Care given and care returned — supporting those who raised you, and raising those in your care well.

Teachers and students

The south

Respect and attention offered to those who teach you; thoroughness and honesty offered to those you teach.

Partner and family

The west

Faithfulness, courtesy and shared responsibility with the person you share a life with.

Friends and companions

The north

Generosity, kind speech, keeping your word, and standing by people when their circumstances turn.

Those who work for you

Below

Work fairly assigned, fairly paid and fairly rested — and honest, careful work given in return.

Those who teach the Dhamma

Above

Support and respect for monastics and teachers; guidance and instruction given back freely.

Sigālovāda Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya 31. The six directions are the sutta's own structure, not a modern rearrangement of it.

The four qualities to grow in

The brahmavihāras — four states of mind cultivated towards others, and the answer the tradition gives to the worry that any teaching might divide people from one another. Each is deliberately boundless: they are practised towards everyone, without exception for nationality, belief or culture.

Mettā — loving-kindness. Wishing others well, beginning with those it is easy for and extending outwards until no one is left out.

Karuṇā — compassion. The mind's response to suffering, wherever it is met.

Muditā — sympathetic joy. Gladness at another's good fortune, which is the direct antidote to envy.

Upekkhā — equanimity. Steadiness that does not depend on things going your way.

✅ What you do not need

Everything on this page can be taken up starting today. To practise it you do not need:

  • to speak Thai, Pali, or any language other than your own
  • to live near a temple, or to have ever visited one
  • to adopt the customs, dress or manners of another country
  • to change your nationality, identity or community
  • to belong to any tradition, culture or group — including no culture at all
  • to call yourself a Buddhist, or to call yourself anything
  • a ceremony, a certificate, or anyone's permission

The Pali terms are here because they are the tradition's precise vocabulary and are worth knowing — not because the practice requires the language.

Where the rest of this site fits

If you want only what is on this page, you have everything you need — go on to the teachings and to meditation, and stop there. Nothing further is required of you. That includes taking on a culture: you can keep the one you already have, borrow from another, or hold to none, and none of those choices changes what is available to you here.

If you are curious about how one particular tradition has carried these teachings — its chanting, its temples, its language and its customs — that is what the Thai side of this site is for, offered as context rather than a condition.

Wherever you are is where the practice happens. 🙏
