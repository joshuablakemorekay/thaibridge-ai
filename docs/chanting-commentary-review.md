## คำบูชาพระรัตนตรัย — Words of Offering to the Triple Gem

*page 1 · English is a working translation*  
`kham-bucha-phra-ratanattaya`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The opening offering of the service: the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha are named by their epithets, and gifts are set out before them.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted at the opening of the morning and evening services, with the offerings already set out before the shrine.

**Historical background**

This is the first chant in the book, and the book prints three headings above it — ระเบียบ, ทำวัตร - สวดมนต์, ทำวัตรเช้า — which name the morning service as a whole rather than this chant. They belong to the page rather than to the text and are recorded in the page note.

บูชา, from the Pali pūjā, is honouring done by an act rather than by a feeling: something is brought and set down. The gifts of the fifth line — สักกาเรหิ, with these gifts — are the candles, incense and flowers arranged before the shrine, and the chant is spoken over them once they are in place. The wording is the tradition's own; the epithets it uses are canonical, but the arrangement into an offering formula is the work of the liturgy that built this service.

The book prints no Thai rendering here, so the chant stands at two of the five layers: the Pali as it is chanted, and an English translation written for this edition rather than taken from the book. The page closes with an instruction to the leader, who invites the assembly to make the offering and chant the Namo; that instruction is a rubric and is kept in the page note.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first three clauses do not address anyone. They identify. Each of the Three Jewels is named by the epithet that belongs to it — the Buddha as arahaṃ sammāsambuddho, worthy and perfectly enlightened by himself; the Dhamma as svākkhāto, well proclaimed; the Sangha as supaṭipanno, having practised well. Only in the fourth clause are the three gathered together, and only in the fifth does anything happen: we make offering with these gifts, set out as befits them.

The second movement turns and asks. It grants at once what might otherwise be passed over — สุจิระปะรินิพพุโตปิ, though the Blessed One long ago attained final Nibbāna, the complete quenching at the end of an awakened life — and asks anyway, on the ground of his compassion for later generations. The gifts are described in the same candid register: ทุคคะตะปัณณาการะภูเต, poor gifts though they are. Neither the distance nor the smallness of the offering is hidden.

That candour is the point of the chant. It is not an attempt to reach someone, and it does not claim that the offering is worth much. What is being arranged is the assembly: a room in which gifts have been set out and this has been said aloud is a room in which a service can begin. The closing line names who benefits, and it is not the one receiving — for our lasting welfare and happiness.

## คำนมัสการพระรัตนตรัย — Words of Salutation to the Triple Gem

*page 1 · English is a working translation*  
`kham-namatsakan-phra-ratanattaya`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Three lines of salutation, one to each of the Three Jewels, each followed by a bow made by the assembly together.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted immediately after the opening offering in the morning and evening services, and often on its own as a brief act of homage.

**Historical background**

The three lines are built from the same three phrases as the offering that precedes them — อะระหัง สัมมาสัมพุทโธ, สวากขาโต and สุปะฏิปันโน. Those phrases belong to the standard recollections of the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha and recur widely in the canon; what the tradition composed here is the arrangement of them into three salutations short enough to bow after.

The bows are printed. Each line is followed by (กราบพร้อมกัน), bow together, set on its own centred line — an instruction to the chanters rather than words to be chanted, and it is what gives this chant its shape: three statements, three prostrations. It is recorded in this file as a rubric rather than as a verse, since nobody says it aloud.

**Meaning and purpose**

Each line has two halves, and they are grammatically unlike. The first half states something in the third person: the Blessed One is worthy and perfectly enlightened by himself; the Dhamma is well proclaimed; the Sangha of his disciples has practised well. Nothing is asked and nothing is claimed. Only in the second half does a speaker appear, and then only to say what they are doing.

The three verbs of that second half are not the same word three times. อะภิวาเทมิ is the salutation made to a person, นะมัสสามิ the homage paid to what is revered, นะมามิ the plain act of bowing. A translation flattens them into one gesture; the Pali keeps three, and the difference tracks the difference between a teacher, a teaching and a community.

The chant is short because it is not principally words. Its purpose is the three bows, and the lines exist to give each bow something to be about — a reason stated immediately before the body performs it. Coming where it does, it settles an assembly into the posture of the service before anything longer begins.

**What it is.** The leader's invitation: having gone for refuge and made the offering, the assembly is called to praise the Blessed One and chant the salutation.

**When it is chanted.** Spoken by the leader of the assembly once the offering has been made, to bring the others into the preliminary salutation that follows it.

**Historical background**

คำเชิญ means an invitation, and this is one of a family of lines in the book that belong to whoever is leading rather than to everyone. They are the joints of a service: one voice says what is about to happen, and the assembly takes up the chant named. This one is longer than most because it also gathers up what has just been done.

The wording places the speakers precisely. They are those who have gone for refuge, who have ปัพพะชิตา — gone forth, left the household life — for the Blessed One's sake, and who live the พ๎รัห๎มะจะริยัง, the holy life, under him as teacher. The chant is therefore phrased for a monastic assembly, which is worth knowing before it is read as everybody's words.

No Thai rendering is printed for this chant, so it stands at the Pali and an English translation written for this edition rather than taken from the book.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first two clauses say nothing about the Buddha and everything about the relation to him. He is the one they went to for refuge, the one for whose sake they went forth, their teacher, the one under whom the holy life is lived. Four descriptions, none of them a quality of his — each is a position the speakers themselves occupy.

The middle clauses then narrate the service back to itself: the offering made with these gifts, set out as befits them, and the respects paid. Only after that comes หันทะทานิ, now then, and with it the turn to what is next — to extol the Blessed One in words, and to make the ปุพพะภาคะนะมะการัง, the preliminary salutation, which is the chant printed immediately below.

What the invitation marks is a change of medium. Up to this point the service has been conducted with the hands and the body: gifts carried in, arranged, bowed to. From here it is carried in speech. A line that says so aloud keeps an assembly of many people moving together, and it gives the reason the book prints this text at all — it is not a chant to be meditated on, but the hinge that lets the next one begin.

## The Preliminary Salutation

`pubbabhaga-namakara`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The single line of homage to the Blessed One, chanted three times, which opens almost every other chant in the book.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted three times before nearly any formal recitation, and before a talk, the taking of the precepts, or an ordination.

**Historical background**

The book prints no title above this chant. The name used here is taken from the word ปุพพะภาคะนะมะการัง in the invitation on the same page, and title_thai and title_pali are left empty rather than invented.

It is also printed three times in full rather than marked as a repeat, and entered here the same way, with an instruction beside it that the deputy leader begins and the others join, three times, pausing for one breath after each. The book is specific about the breath because the line is chanted by a room rather than a person, and a shared pause is what keeps a room together.

This is very likely the most often recited sentence in Theravāda Buddhism. In the canon it is the phrase spoken by those declaring their confidence in the Buddha, and it appears in a great many places, which is exactly why no single reference is given for it above.

**Meaning and purpose**

The line contains no request, no doctrine and no promise. It is one word of homage followed by its object: นะโม, homage, and then the Blessed One under three epithets — ภะคะวะโต, the fortunate or blessed one; อะระหะโต, worthy, one in whom the defilements are ended; สัมมาสัมพุทธัสสะ, perfectly enlightened by himself, that is, awakened without being taught it by anyone.

The grammar keeps its distance. ตัสสะ means that one — the Buddha is spoken about, not spoken to. Nothing in the sentence supposes a presence in the room or expects an answer, and this is one of the reasons it can be said equally at a funeral, before a lesson, and at the start of an ordinary morning.

Its work is to mark a threshold. Said three times, it separates ordinary speech from chanting, so that whatever follows begins already inside the act rather than drifting into it. For most learners it is also the first Pali they meet, and the sentence by which the rest of the language is later recognised.

## In Praise of the Buddha

*page 2 · English is a working translation*  
`buddhabhithuti`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Praise of the Buddha by his epithets and by what he did — made this world known, taught the Dhamma, and proclaimed the holy life entire.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the morning service, the first of three praises given in turn to the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha.

**Historical background**

The book prints no title above this chant. The name used here is taken from the word พุทธาภิถุติง in its own invitation line — หันทะ มะยัง พุทธาภิถุติง กะโรมะ เส, now let us give praise to the Buddha — which the leader says to open it. title_thai and title_pali are left empty rather than invented.

The material is not new. The string of epithets belongs to the standard recollection of the Buddha, and the passages about making this world known and teaching a Dhamma admirable in beginning, middle and end are stock formulas that recur widely in the canon. What the liturgy composed is the frame: the relative pronoun โย โส, that one who, opened at the start and left unresolved across every clause that follows.

No Thai rendering is printed here either, so this chant too stands at the Pali and an English translation written for this edition rather than taken from the book.

**Meaning and purpose**

The whole chant is one sentence. It opens with โย โส ตะถาคะโต, that Tathāgata — the term the Buddha used of himself rather than a title given to him — and then withholds its main verb through eight clauses of description before landing, in the ninth, on what the reciter does about it. Read as English it looks like a list. Chanted, it is a single held breath of a sentence that has to arrive somewhere.

What is praised comes in two kinds. First the epithets: accomplished in knowledge and conduct, well-gone, knower of worlds, unexcelled trainer of those who can be taught, teacher of gods and men. Then the deeds, which are more specific — he made this world known with its gods, its Māras and its Brahmās, Māra being the figure of death and temptation and Brahmā a god of the higher heavens; he did so having realised it himself through direct knowledge, not on anyone's authority; and he taught in a way that was complete in meaning and in letter both.

The sentence ends in two acts rather than a conclusion: to that Blessed One I make offering, to that Blessed One I bow my head. That is the reason the chant survives in daily use. It is not recited to inform anyone of the Buddha's qualities, which the assembly already knows, but so that the bow at the end is made by someone who has just said, at length and out loud, what the bow is for.

**Historical background**

The book prints no title above this chant. The name used here is taken from the word ธัมมาภิถุติง in its own invitation line, and title_thai and title_pali are left empty rather than invented.

It is the middle panel of a set of three, and the three are built to the same plan: โย โส, that one which, followed by the qualities, and closing on ตะมะหัง — to that I make offering, to that I bow my head. Read alone it looks abrupt at three verses; read in place it is one third of a single movement, and its brevity is what sets off the nine clauses given to the Buddha.

The qualities themselves are the standard recollection of the Dhamma and recur widely in the canon; what is composed here is their setting as praise. No Thai rendering is printed, so the chant stands at the Pali and an English translation written for this edition.

**Meaning and purpose**

Six qualities are named, and they describe the teaching by what can be done with it rather than by what it asserts. สันทิฏฐิโก, visible here and now — its results are met in this life, not in a report about another. อะกาลิโก, timeless, not waiting on a season or an age. เอหิปัสสิโก, inviting all to come and see, which is an unusual thing for a teaching to say of itself. โอปะนะยิโก, leading onwards, taking whoever follows it somewhere.

The sixth is the one the others rest on: ปัจจัตตัง เวทิตัพโพ วิญญูหิ, to be known each for himself by the wise. The knowledge is not transferable. Nobody can hold it on another's behalf, which is why the list ends there, and why an assembly can chant this together without any of them speaking for the rest.

That is also why it is still recited. The verse is not information about the Dhamma — an assembly chanting it daily knows the six words already. It is a description read back over one's own practice, and each recitation is an occasion to ask whether what is being practised still answers to it.

## In Praise of the Sangha

*page 3 · English is a working translation*  
`sanghabhithuti`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Praise of the Sangha by the four ways it has practised and the four kinds of honour owed it, closing with an offering and a bow.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the morning service, the last of the three praises given in turn to the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha.

**Historical background**

The book prints no title here either; the name is taken from the word สังฆาภิถุติง in the invitation, and title_thai and title_pali are left empty rather than invented. The chant is followed by a fuller rubric than the two before it — bow together, then all sit in the side-resting posture — which marks the close of the whole opening movement rather than of this chant alone.

สังโฆ here does not mean the monastic community in the ordinary sense. The fifth line defines it: the four pairs, the eight types of noble ones — those who have reached each of the four stages of awakening and those established in them. Membership is by attainment rather than by ordination, and the praise is addressed to that body wherever its members happen to be.

**Meaning and purpose**

The chant opens with four ways of practising, laid out in a single repeated frame. สุปะฏิปันโน, having practised well; อุชุปะฏิปันโน, straightforwardly; ญายะปะฏิปันโน, methodically, by the right method; สามีจิปะฏิปันโน, masterfully, as is fitting. These are not four groups but four aspects of one practice, and the whole phrase is repeated each time so that no shorthand creeps in.

The second half turns from what the Sangha has done to what is therefore owed it: worthy of gifts, of hospitality, of offerings, of respect. It closes on the phrase that explains all four — อะนุตตะรัง ปุญญักเขตตัง โลกัสสะ, the unexcelled field of merit for the world. The image is agricultural: a field is not honoured for its own sake but because what is planted in it grows.

That image is the practical point of the chant, and it is why a lay assembly recites it as readily as a monastic one. It gives an account of why supporting a community of practitioners is worth doing, in terms of what such support produces rather than of obligation. The chant then does what it has just described, and makes its own offering and bow.

## ระตะนัตตะยัปปะณามะคาถา — Verses in Salutation to the Triple Gem

*pages 3–4 · English is a working translation*  
`ratanattayappanamagatha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Sixteen verses of salutation, four each to the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha, closing by dedicating the merit of having made them.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the morning service after the three praises, sharing one invitation with the passage on urgency that follows it.

**Historical background**

This is the first chant on these pages whose title the book actually prints, centred and in bold. It is also the first in metre rather than prose: where the praises before it accumulate clauses, these move in stanzas of four lines — three of description and one of response.

The verses are a composition rather than a canonical passage, and their vocabulary is more ornamented than the formulas that precede them: the Buddha as กะรุณามะหัณณะโว, a great ocean of compassion, the Dhamma as ปะทีโป วิยะ, like a lamp. The figures are the work of a poet drawing on the tradition rather than a text being quoted.

The three section labels inside this chant are editorial, added for this edition. The book separates the salutations by layout alone and prints no headings between them.

**Meaning and purpose**

Each of the three salutations is built the same way: three lines of description, then the same closing line with one word changed — วันทามิ พุทธัง, วันทามิ ธัมมัง, วันทามิ สังฆัง, I revere that Buddha, that Dhamma, that Sangha, อะหะมาทะเรนะ, with devotion. The repetition makes the three parallel without stating that they are, and a reciter arrives at the same act three times having come by three different roads.

The images are chosen to say what each one does. The Buddha is an ocean of compassion whose eye of knowledge is purified — capacity and sight. The Dhamma is a lamp: not the destination but what makes the way visible, and the verse then divides it into path, fruit and the Deathless — the practice, its results, and what lies beyond both. The Sangha is a field surpassing all fields, and those in it have seen peace and awakened after the Well-Gone One, followers rather than originators.

The last stanza changes register entirely. Having made the salutations, it asks that the merit of having made them clear away obstacles. This is a dedication, and it is why the chant sits where it does: the reverence just offered is not left as a feeling but turned into something with a use, so that the passage which follows can begin from a mind already settled.

## สังเวคะปะริกิตตะนะปาฐะ — The Passage on Spiritual Urgency

*pages 4–5 · English is a working translation*  
`samvega-parikittana-patha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** A long passage stating what the Buddha taught about suffering and impermanence, and why those reciting it have taken up the training.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the morning service directly after the verses of salutation, with which it shares a single invitation.

**Historical background**

สังเวคะ, saṃvega, has no comfortable English equivalent. It names the jolt of recognising that the situation is serious and that time is limited — not despair, which stops a person, but the thing that starts one. The title says the passage exists to bring that about, which is unusual: most chants say what they are about rather than what they are for.

The passage shares its invitation with the verses before it — one line on page 3 covers both — so the two are chanted as a single unbroken stretch, and this chant's own invitation is left empty in the file rather than duplicated.

Much of the wording is canonical and will be recognised: the statement of the first noble truth, the list of the five aggregates, and the formulas of impermanence and not-self. What the liturgy has done is set them in the first person plural and close them with a statement of the reciters' own position.

**Meaning and purpose**

The passage moves in three stages. First it establishes the situation: a Tathāgata has arisen and a Dhamma has been taught that leads out. Then it states what has been learned from it — birth, ageing and death are ทุกขา, suffering, and so is being joined to what is disliked, parted from what is liked, and not getting what one wants; in short, the five aggregates of clinging are suffering. The five are then named one by one, unhurried.

The second stage repeats those same five twice more, first as อะนิจจัง, impermanent, then as อะนัตตา, not-self — not one's own, not a self, not something a person finally is. The repetition is deliberate and slow. The passage is not arguing the point; it goes through the list item by item, in a way that gives the recognition time to land.

The third stage brings it home to the people chanting. เต มะยัง — all of us — are beset by birth, ageing and death and overcome by suffering; and then comes the line that turns the whole passage: perhaps an end to this whole mass of suffering might be found. Everything after it is consequence. It is because that possibility exists that the reciters have gone forth, live the holy life and have taken up the training, and the closing line asks that the life so taken up should lead where it was undertaken to lead.

It is chanted daily rather than studied because its work is not to establish anything new. It is a statement of position, made aloud every morning, that keeps the reason for the day's practice from quietly going missing.

## ตังขะณิกะปัจจะเวกขะณะปาโฐ — The Reflection at the Moment of Use

*pages 6–7 · English is a working translation*  
`tangkhanika-paccavekkhana-patho`

**Source (printed in the book):** นัย ม. มู ๑๒/๑๓-๘

**What it is.** Four reflections on the four requisites — robe, almsfood, lodging and medicine — each stating the proper reason for using it.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the morning service, and used again by monastics at the moment a requisite is actually taken up.

**Historical background**

Unlike the chants around it, this one carries a reference printed in the book itself — นัย ม. มู ๑๒/๑๓-๘ — so its source is the book's own, and nothing has been attributed on top of it.

ตังขะณิกะ means at that moment, and the title is doing real work. This is not a reflection on requisites in general but one made at the instant of use: while putting on the robe, before eating, on entering the lodging, when taking medicine. Chanting it in the morning rehearses a form meant to be used again during the day.

The four section labels are editorial, added for this edition. The book separates the reflections by a blank line only and prints no headings between them.

**Meaning and purpose**

Each of the four opens the same way — ปะฏิสังขา โยนิโส, reflecting wisely — then names its requisite and gives its purposes. The construction that follows is the important one: ยาวะเทวะ, only so far as, for just this much and no more. The robe is for warding off cold and heat and the touch of gadflies, mosquitoes, wind, sun and creeping things, and for covering the body. A limit is set before a use is granted.

The purposes given are deliberately modest and physical. Nothing is said about the quality of the robe, the taste of the food or the comfort of the lodging; the reflections name only what the requisite is needed for. The effect is to leave the thing in use while removing the reasons for wanting a better one, without requiring the practitioner to go without.

This is why it survives as a daily chant rather than a rule. A rule can be kept while the wanting continues underneath it; a reflection made at the moment of use puts the question in the middle of the act itself, where it can actually be answered. Recited by an assembly each morning, it keeps the form ready for the occasions during the day when it is meant to be used.

## ปัตติทานะคาถา — The Verses for Sharing Merit

*pages 7–8 · English is a working translation*  
`pattidana-gatha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Verses sharing the merit of the service with the devas and with all beings, closing in a blessing on the Dhamma, the Sangha, the rains and those who rule.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted at the very end of the morning service, after which the book marks the service complete.

**Historical background**

ปัตติทานะ is the giving over of merit — puñña, the good accumulated by an action. The tradition holds that merit is not spent by being shared, so a service ends by handing on what it has just made rather than by keeping it. Almost every formal chanting in this book closes with some form of this.

It is the last chant of the morning service, and the book says so on the page: below the final verse it prints จบพิธีทำวัตรเช้า, centred and bold. That line closes the whole service rather than this chant, so it has no field here and is kept in the page note.

The chant falls into two movements of different character, and the second begins at ฐาตุ จิรัง. No Thai rendering is printed, so the chant stands at the Pali and an English translation written for this edition.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first movement addresses the devas of the place — those dwelling in the stupa, the dwelling and the Bodhi enclosure, here and there — and what is offered them is ธัมมะทาเนนะ, the gift of Dhamma. Not goods, and not praise: the merit of the service just completed. What is asked in return is modest, that they bring safety within the monastery.

It then widens in a deliberate order, and the order is the argument. Elder, middling and newly ordained bhikkhus; then monastery helpers, donors and lay followers; then villages, lands, towns and their rulers; then all living beings; and finally the four ways of being born — from the womb, from eggs, from moisture, or spontaneously arisen. By the end nothing that lives has been left outside the sentence.

The closing movement stops sharing and starts blessing, and it asks for ordinary conditions rather than spiritual ones: that the Dhamma stand long and the Sangha be united, that rain fall in season and the earth come to plenty, and that rulers protect their people as a mother and father protect their own child. This is why the chant is kept for the end. A service that closed on its own praise would finish facing inward; this one finishes by turning what was made in the room outward, to the weather and the government and everyone not present.

## The Passage on Spiritual Urgency — lay chanters' version

*page 8 · English is a working translation*  
`samvega-parikittana-patha-lay-variant`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The closing of the passage on urgency, reworded for lay chanters who have gone for refuge rather than gone forth.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted by lay people in place of the closing lines of the passage on urgency, which are phrased for those who have gone forth.

**Historical background**

This is a variant rather than a chant of its own. The book prints it not among the chants but inside the explanatory section on the morning service, alongside notes on which words lay chanters substitute.

It exists because the parent passage ends in words only a monastic can say honestly. Its closing lines describe having gone forth from home into homelessness and having taken up the training and livelihood of the bhikkhus — a description of a life, not a wish. A lay chanter reciting them would be describing somebody else.

The book prints no Thai rendering and no title for it. The title and identifier used in this app are the app's own.

**Meaning and purpose**

The substitution is precise. Where the monastic version has the reciters going forth for the sake of the Blessed One, this has them going to him for refuge, and to the Dhamma and the Bhikkhu Sangha with him. Refuge is available to anyone; going forth is not. Everything else in the sentence is left standing.

The phrase that carries the change is ยะถาสัตติ ยะถาพะลัง, as far as ability and strength allow. It is not a lowering of the standard but a statement of scope, and it is what makes the sentence sayable by someone with a household, work and obligations that a monastic does not have.

The last line is then identical to the monastic one, word for word: that this practice lead to the ending of this whole mass of suffering. That is the point of printing the variant at all. What is adjusted is the description of the life; the aim it is pointed at is not adjusted, and the book takes some trouble to show that it is not.

## The Elaborate Invitation to Revere the Triple Gem

*page 9 · English is a working translation*  
`kham-choen-bucha-phitsadan`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The long form of the opening invitation, used on observance days, which stops to explain why an image of the Buddha stands where the offerings are placed.

**When it is chanted.** Spoken by the leader in place of the ordinary invitation on observance days (วันธรรมสวนะ) and on Sundays.

**Historical background**

This is the พิสดาร, the elaborate, form of an invitation whose ordinary form stands earlier in the book. The two open with the same words and then part company, and they are kept as two chants here rather than merged.

What makes it elaborate is not ornament but explanation. The short form goes from refuge to offering in a handful of clauses. This one takes eighteen, because between those two points it stops to give an account of the shrine that the offering is being made at.

It is unusual in naming its own object. **Meaning and purpose**

Transmission has been uneven, and the chant says so.

The middle explains the shrine, and it does so as a chain of permissions. Out of compassion for later generations the Blessed One allowed a shrine to be established after his passing; on the strength of that permission this image was made; and its purpose is then stated exactly — solely so that, seeing it and calling him to mind, one may gain confidence and a sense of urgency, and on that account fulfil the right practice. The image is described as an aid to recollection, and nothing more is claimed for it.

Only after all that does the chant do what the short form does in a line: offerings placed on the lion-throne, homage paid, and the assembly handed on to the preliminary salutation. That order is the reason it is kept for observance days. On a day when people come who may not come often, the room raises an obvious question about what the image is for, and this invitation answers it before anyone is asked to bow to it.

## คำประกาศอุโบสถ — The Announcement of the Uposatha

*page 10 · English is a working translation*  
`kham-prakat-ubosot`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The announcement made before the eight precepts are undertaken, stating that today is an observance day and calling those present to keep it.

**When it is chanted.** Spoken on the observance day before the precepts are requested, to tell the assembly what day it is and what is about to be undertaken.

**Historical background**

อุโบสถ, Uposatha, is the observance day that falls at the quarters of the lunar month. On it lay people may undertake eight precepts rather than the usual five, for one night and one day, and spend the day at the monastery hearing the Dhamma. This passage is the announcement made before any of that begins.

The Thai printed with it is not a translation. The book sets the whole Pali as one block and the whole คำแปล as another, and the Thai is a free and much expanded rendering — it opens with a preamble that has no Pali behind it at all, and where the Pali says only that each abstention should be made the object of the mind, the Thai spells out all eight of them one by one. The pairing of Pali units to Thai chunks in this edition was made by meaning rather than by line.

The two layers also sit on different pages: all the Pali is on page 10, while the Thai runs over onto page 11.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first line does something no other chant in this part of the book does — it states the date. Today is the fifteenth day of the lunar fortnight. The observance is fixed to the calendar rather than to inclination, and saying the date aloud is what separates this day from the one before it.

The second and third lines give the reason and then the invitation: such a day is the time appointed by the Buddha for hearing the Dhamma, and for that same purpose the time of the Uposatha for laymen and laywomen; come then, let all of us gathered here keep it for this night and this day. The period is bounded on purpose. What is being proposed is a day, not a change of life.

The fourth line is the method — mark out the period, make each abstention the object of the mind, keep the mind undistracted — and the fifth is the sentence people remember: for to us who have reached such an Uposatha as this, may our life not be barren of fruit. That is why the announcement is made at all. It puts an ordinary day on the record as one that could be spent or used.

## คำอาราธนาอุโบสถศีล — The Request for the Uposatha Precepts

*page 11 · English is a working translation*  
`kham-aradhana-ubosot-sila`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The request made to a monk for the eight-factored Uposatha together with the Three Refuges, spoken three times.

**When it is chanted.** Spoken by the lay assembly on the observance day, after the announcement and before the precepts are administered.

**Historical background**

คำอาราธนา means a request or an invitation, and the word sets out the shape of what happens next. Precepts in this tradition are not handed down unasked. Someone who wants them asks a monk for them, and only then are they given, line by line, to be repeated.

What is asked for is one thing in two parts: the Uposatha อัฏฐังคะสะมันนาคะตัง, endowed with eight factors, ติสะระเณนะ สะหะ, together with the Three Refuges. Refuge and precepts are requested in a single breath rather than as two transactions.

All three repetitions are printed in full. The book does not shorten the second and third, and neither does this edition. It is printed in Pali only, so the English is a working translation made here rather than the book's.

**Meaning and purpose**

Almost nothing changes across the three lines. Only ทุติยัมปิ and ตะติยัมปิ are added — for a second time, for a third time. Saying a thing three times is the tradition's way of marking that it has been formally done rather than merely said, and the same device appears elsewhere in this book wherever something needs to be unmistakable.

The grammar is collective and it is a request, not a declaration. มะยัง, we, and ยาจามะ, we ask. Nothing has been undertaken at this point. The assembly has asked to be given the chance to undertake it, and the precepts themselves come afterwards.

That order is the whole substance of the chant, and the reason it is worth its three lines. A precept that is asked for belongs to the person who asked; one that is simply administered can be received without ever having been chosen. The request is short because it only has to establish who is doing the choosing.

## The Going to the Three Refuges, for the Uposatha

*page 12 · English is a working translation*  
`tisarana-gamana-ubosot`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The threefold homage followed by the threefold going for refuge, taken before the eight precepts on the observance day.

**When it is chanted.** Recited on the observance day after the request for the precepts, repeated line by line after the monk who gives them.

**Historical background**

Going for refuge is the act by which a person becomes a follower of this tradition rather than an admirer of it. Refuge here does not mean rescue: what is taken is not shelter from difficulty but a direction — the Buddha as the one who found the way, the Dhamma as the way itself, and the Sangha as those who have walked it.

The book prints no title for the passage; the name used in this app records where it sits, in the sequence for the observance day. The same words are used at every other occasion when refuge is taken, so nothing here is peculiar to the Uposatha except its position.

The homage is printed in full at the head, three times, and each refuge is then said three times over. Nothing is abbreviated, and the file follows the page.

**Meaning and purpose**

The central word is a verb. คัจฉามิ — I go. Not I believe, not I accept, not I agree with. Something is being done rather than assented to, and it is done in the first person singular: in a hall of a hundred people, each one says I.

The second and third rounds are marked only by ทุติยัมปิ and ตะติยัมปิ, for a second time and for a third. The words are otherwise unchanged. Saying a thing three times is how this tradition marks that it has been formally done and not merely spoken, and the device recurs throughout the book.

Its position explains why it matters. The precepts that follow are undertaken by somebody who has just said where they stand. Without the refuge, eight rules kept for a day would be a set of resolutions; with it, they are the shape of a day given to something in particular.

## อุโบสถศีล — The Eight Uposatha Precepts

*page 12 · English is a working translation*  
`ubosot-sila`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The eight precepts undertaken for a single night and day of the observance, with the closing line that binds them to that period.

**When it is chanted.** Undertaken on the observance day after the refuges, repeated line by line after the monk who administers them.

**Historical background**

Lay people ordinarily keep five precepts. อุโบสถศีล is the eight kept on the observance day instead, and the difference is not simply three more rules. The third is changed rather than added to — abstaining from unchastity altogether, in place of the usual rule against misconduct — and the three that follow are new.

Every line has the same ending: เวระมะณี สิกขาปะทัง สะมาทิยามิ, I undertake the training rule to abstain from. A สิกขาปะทัง is a training rule, not a commandment, and สะมาทิยามิ means I take it up. Nobody imposes these; they are picked up by the person keeping them and put down again at the end of the day.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first five are the familiar ones: not taking life, not taking what is not given, not unchastity, not false speech, no intoxicants. The three that follow are of a different kind altogether — no eating at the wrong time, no dancing, singing, music, shows, garlands, scents or adornment, and no high or large bed. Nobody is harmed by a soft mattress, and that is precisely the point of including it.

These three are there to make the day feel unlike other days. Take away the afternoon meal, the entertainment and the comfortable bed, and what is left is a great deal of time and very little to fill it with, which is the condition the observance is arranged to produce. It is a narrowing for a purpose, not a verdict on ordinary comforts.

The ninth line is what keeps the whole thing honest. It names what is being undertaken — this Uposatha endowed with eight factors and appointed by the Buddha — and then bounds it: for this night and this day. The undertaking has its ending written into it, which is what makes it something a person with a household can actually promise.

**When it is chanted.** Spoken once by the monk immediately after the eight precepts have been undertaken.

One sentence, and it belongs to the giver rather than to those receiving. The exchange on these pages has a definite shape — the request, the refuges, the precepts, and then this line, which closes it. The book prints no title; the name used here is taken from its opening words.

**Meaning and purpose**

The line names what has just happened and says what is now to be done with it: these eight training rules, well undertaken as the virtue of the observance, are to be guarded — อัปปะมาเทนะ, with heedfulness. The word is worth pausing on. Its opposite is not wickedness but inattention, and the tradition treats inattention as the way most undertakings are actually lost.

It gives the reasons first and the instruction last, so that a person who has just undertaken eight precepts is told why before being told what.

## สะระณะคะมะนานุสสะระณะคาถา — Verses Recollecting the Going for Refuge

*page 13 · English is a working translation*  
`sarana-gamananussarana-gatha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Verses recalling that one has gone for refuge and declared oneself a lay follower, and resolving to practise as far as strength allows.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted after the refuges and precepts have been taken, as a recollection of what has just been undertaken.

**Historical background**

The title names it a recollection, and that is the distinction worth holding on to: this is not the going for refuge but the calling of it to mind afterwards. The tradition treats such recollection as a practice in its own right rather than as a commemoration.

**Meaning and purpose**

The claim in the middle is a large one, stated without qualification: this is my safe refuge, this is the supreme refuge, and coming to it one is freed from all suffering. เขมัง means safe in the sense of secure, out of danger. Nothing is hedged.

The last two verses then measure what the speaker can actually promise, and the contrast is deliberate. ยะถาพะลัง — as far as my strength allows — is the same qualification the book uses elsewhere when a chant is put into lay mouths. What is claimed for the refuge is unlimited; what is claimed for the person is not.

That pairing is the reason the verses are recited rather than simply known. Between them the two halves say that the refuge is complete and the practitioner is not, and the chant closes looking forward — อะนาคะเต, in time to come — rather than on anything already achieved.

## คำขอขมาพระรัตนตรัย — The Request for Forgiveness from the Triple Gem

*page 14 · English is a working translation*  
`kham-kho-khama-phra-rattanatrai`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** A request for pardon for any wrong done by body, speech or mind towards the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha, made to each in turn.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted before undertaking a period of practice or an important occasion, and commonly at the close of the day's chanting.

**Historical background**

ขอขมา is to ask pardon. The chant is a single stanza said three times with only its object changed — the Buddha, then the Dhamma, then the Sangha — which is the same shape as several of the salutations earlier in the book.

What is asked for is narrower than it may appear. This tradition has no mechanism by which a fault is cancelled because it has been confessed, and the chant does not ask for one. What the formula does is bring a fault into the open and make a statement about the future.

**Meaning and purpose**

Each stanza has four movements. First the three doors through which anything at all is done — กาเยนะ วาจายะ วะ เจตะสา วา, by body, by speech, or by mind. Then the fault. Then the request that it be accepted. Then the reason for asking, which is the line that carries the weight.

That reason is กาลันตะเร สังวะริตุง — that in time to come I may be restrained. The pardon is asked for the sake of future restraint, not for relief from the past, and nothing in the chant asks for anything to be undone. What is sought is permission to go on rather than absolution for having stumbled.

Its use follows from that. Someone about to begin something — a retreat, a residence, a day of precepts — starts by naming what has gone wrong instead of carrying it unspoken. The chant is short because its work is only to get the thing said.

## เสขิยวัตร — The Sekhiya Rules of Training

*pages 15–16 · English is a working translation*  
`sekhiyavatta`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The first group of the training observances — twenty-six rules on how a monk wears the robe, walks and sits among houses.

**When it is chanted.** Recited by monastics after the morning service, following the book's own instruction, as a periodic review of the training rules.

**Historical background**

เสขิยวัตร are the training observances, the last and largest section of the monastic rule and also the mildest. Falling short of one is not an offence calling for any formal remedy. They govern deportment — how a monk appears to the households he walks among every morning.

The book divides them into four groups and this is the first, สารุปปะ, on what is fitting, with twenty-six rules. Its heading and count are the book's own, printed at the head of the group.

It is printed in Pali only, so the English here is a working translation made for this edition. Several of the terms in this group are technical and carry established renderings in translations of the monastic code, which would repay comparison before anyone leans on the English.

**Meaning and purpose**

Every rule is built the same way: a statement in the first person about what one will do, closing with สิกขา กะระณียา, this is a training to be observed. It is not a prohibition issued to somebody. It is a sentence the monk says about himself, and the grammar turns each rule into a resolution rather than an order.

What they cover is strikingly ordinary. The lower robe and the upper robe worn evenly all round; going among the houses well covered; not walking on the heels or on tiptoe; not sitting lolling or clasping the knees. None of it is morality. All of it is about being unremarkable in someone else's street.

That is why they are worth reciting. A community that eats by alms lives on the goodwill of the villages it walks through, and a monk who is careless in a doorway is not committing a wrong so much as spending something that is not only his. Saying the list aloud keeps a long catalogue of small things where it can actually act — in the memory, before the moment arrives.

**When it is chanted.** Recited by monastics after the morning service, with the other three groups of the training observances.

**Historical background**

**Meaning and purpose**

The group opens with attention rather than restriction. Almsfood is to be received สักกัจจัง, attentively; with attention on the bowl; with a due proportion of curry. Nothing is said about how much food or how good it is. What is governed is the manner of receiving it.

The later rules become specific to the point of seeming trivial — not taking a water vessel with a hand soiled by food, not throwing out bowl-rinsing water with rice grains in it among the houses. Read as a legal code that is odd. Read as a description of somebody eating carefully in another person's home, it is ordinary good manners written down and committed to memory.

The meal is the one moment of the day when a monk is wholly dependent and wholly visible, receiving from people who may have little. These rules take that exposure and make it something practised in advance rather than handled on the spot.

## The Sekhiya Rules — Dhammadesanāpaṭisaṃyutta

**When it is chanted.** Recited by monastics after the morning service, with the other three groups of the training observances.

**Historical background**

The third of the four groups. As with the second, the book prints no title, only the heading with its count, and the English title is the app's own.

One feature runs through every rule and is easy to read past: อะคิลานัสสะ, someone who is not ill. The exception is restated in all sixteen rather than declared once at the head, so that no rule can be quoted without it. Illness suspends the whole group.

**Meaning and purpose**

Each rule names a listener and declines. Someone holding a sunshade, a staff or a knife. Someone walking in front while the teacher walks behind, or on the path while the teacher is off it. The refusal is never about the person's worth — it is about the arrangement they are standing in.

What the group protects is the teaching rather than the teacher. Somebody armed, or striding ahead, or being addressed from below is not placed to receive anything; teaching them in that moment spends the Dhamma on an occasion that cannot hold it. The rules describe the conditions under which speech is worth making.

Taken together they amount to a rule against speaking merely because one has something to say. And the exception says what the whole group is for: where there is real need, every one of these arrangements stops mattering at once.

## ทะสะธัมมะสุตตัง — The Discourse on the Ten Things

*pages 19–20 · chant 1 in the book · English is a working translation*  
`dasadhamma-sutta`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** A discourse listing ten things one who has gone forth should reflect on again and again, from the loss of caste to how the days and nights are passing.

**When it is chanted.** Recited by monastics among the regular reflections, and printed here as the first numbered chant in the book.

**Historical background**

This is the first chant the book gives a number to — '1.' is printed before the title. It is a discourse rather than a composed verse, and it is entered with its whole frame intact: it opens เอวัมเม สุตัง, thus have I heard, gives the setting at Sāvatthi in Jeta's Grove, and closes with the listeners' response.

The ten are the monastic counterpart of the five recollections given to everyone. Where those name what nobody escapes — ageing, sickness, death — these name what somebody has taken on by choice, and can therefore be asked whether they are living up to.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first reflections are about position. One has come to a state without caste; one's life is dependent on others; one's bearing should be other than it was. These describe a change already made and ask whether it has actually been noticed by the person who made it.

Two then turn to conscience and they are deliberately paired: does my own conscience reproach me as to virtue, and do my wise companions in the holy life, having examined me, reproach me. The second is the harder, because it cannot be settled privately.

The last three ask what the life is actually being spent on. How do the days and nights pass for me. Do I delight in empty dwellings. Have I attained anything such that, questioned in my last days, I should not be embarrassed. These are not rhetorical, and the discourse says how to use them: อะภิณ๎หัง, again and again. Their whole work is done by returning to them, which is why the text is chanted rather than read once.

## ปะริตตะกะระณะปาฐะ — The Passage for Making a Protection

*pages 20–22 · chant 2 in the book · English is a working translation*  
`parittakarana-patha`

**Source (written for this edition):** Composite. The verses are those of ระตะนะสุตตัง ทุติยัง, printed at page 82 of this book with the reference ขุ. ขุ. 25/5 ขุ.สุ. 25/367; the prose passages are those of อัคคัปปะสาทะสุตตัง, printed at page 112 with อํ. จตุกฺก. 21/44.

**What it is.** A protection text alternating prose declarations that the Buddha, dispassion and the Sangha are foremost with verses invoking the truth of each.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted when a protection is being made — at a blessing, over a new house, or for someone who is ill.

**Historical background**

ปะริตตะ, paritta, means a protection: a text chanted for safety rather than for instruction. The tradition's own account of how it works is not magical but a matter of truth — a true statement is made, and its truth is then invoked. That is why each verse here ends by naming a truth and asking well-being on the strength of it.

The chant is an assembly rather than a text. Three prose passages, each declaring one thing foremost of its kind, alternate with verses that close on a common refrain. Both halves appear elsewhere in this same book as complete chants in their own right, which is how the source above was established.

**Meaning and purpose**

The three prose passages are built identically. Of all beings, footless, two-footed, four-footed or many-footed, with form or formless, the Tathāgata is declared foremost. Of all things conditioned or unconditioned, dispassion. Of all communities and assemblies, the Sangha of the Tathāgata's disciples. Each is a superlative, and each is then handed over to a verse.

The verses close on the same two moves: this too is an excellent treasure, and by this truth may there be well-being. Nothing is requested as a favour and nobody is petitioned. The well-being is claimed as a consequence of something being the case.

That structure is the reason the chant is used where it is. **When it is chanted.** Recited as a recollection of the Buddha's awakening, the three utterances traditionally placed in the three watches of the night beneath the Bodhi tree.

**Historical background**

These are the first three utterances of the Udāna, spoken in the days immediately following the awakening. The tradition places them in the three watches of a single night, and the book's footnote points to the Udāna together with its Vinaya parallel, where the same verses open the account of the Buddha's first weeks after enlightenment.

The three stanzas are built on repetition. The first two lines are identical in all three, and the third line is identical in the first two, so what changes is only the ending — which is where each stanza's point lies. The book prints the repeated lines out in full rather than abbreviating them.

No Thai rendering is printed on this page, so the entry stands at two of the five layers.

**Meaning and purpose**

The shared opening sets the condition: when things become manifest to a brahmin who is ardent and meditating. The word used for the practitioner is พ์ราห์มะณัสสะ, brahmin — not in the sense of birth or caste, but in the sense the Buddha regularly gave the word, of one who has actually done the work.

The first stanza ends with knowing things together with their causes, สะเหตุธัมมัง. The second ends with knowing the destruction of those conditions, ขะยัง ปัจจะยานัง. These are the two halves of dependent origination, arising and cessation, which is why the book places these verses immediately after the Paṭiccasamuppādapāṭho — the previous chant states the formula, and this one records what it was like to see it.

The third stanza changes shape. Instead of naming something known, it gives an image: he stands scattering the host of Māra, like the sun lighting up the sky. Nothing is added to the doctrine; what is added is what the knowing looks like from outside. The first two stanzas say what was understood, and the third says what such understanding does.

## ภัทเทกะรัตตะคาถา — The Verses on the Auspicious Single Night

*page 33 · chant 16 in the book · English is a working translation*  
`bhaddekaratta-gatha`

**Source (printed in the book):** ม.อุ. 14/348

**What it is.** Do not chase the past or hope for the future; see the present clearly, strive today, for who knows death tomorrow — such a one is called auspicious.

**When it is chanted.** Recited as a reflection on dwelling in the present, and on the urgency of effort in view of death.

**Historical background**

The title has occupied translators for a long time. Bhaddekaratta is literally the auspicious single night, and the verses describe the person who deserves the name rather than defining the term. The tradition treats it as naming an ideal day and night well spent, and the closing stanza gives the epithet its explanation: it is the sage at peace who so declares.

These four stanzas are given in the canon within a discourse of the same name, and are followed there by a detailed exposition of what pursuing the past and hoping for the future consist in. The chanting books print the verses alone. No Thai rendering appears on this page, so the entry stands at two of the five layers and the English is my own off the Pali.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first stanza rules out two directions before naming a third. One should not run back after the past nor place hope in what has not come, and the reason given is bare fact rather than exhortation: what is past is left behind, and the future is not yet arrived. Neither is available to be lived in, which is not a moral claim but a statement about what exists.

The second stanza names what is left. Whatever state is present, one sees it clearly, there where it is — ตัตถะ ตัตถะ, in each case, in each place. Two qualities are asked of that seeing: อะสังหรัง, unshakeable, and อะสังกุปปัง, unwavering. Knowing that, one should cultivate it. The instruction is not to prefer the present as a better object but to see it in a way the other two cannot be seen.

The third stanza supplies the urgency, and does so with a military image. The effort is to be made today, for who knows whether death comes tomorrow — and there is no bargaining with that great host, มะหาเสเนนะ มัจจุนา, Death and his army. The word for bargaining is the language of treaty and truce, which is precisely what cannot be arranged here.

The closing stanza gathers the whole into a description of a person: one who dwells thus, ardent, tireless by day and by night. Him the peaceful sage calls one of the auspicious single night. The verses never define the phrase in the abstract; they build the person first and award the name last.

**What it is.** The Buddha visits the gravely ill Mahākassapa, recites the seven factors of awakening, and Mahākassapa rises from the illness.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted at the bedside of the sick, the first of three such discourses the book prints together.

**Historical background**

โพชฌังคะ are the factors of awakening, seven of them: mindfulness, investigation of states, energy, rapture, tranquillity, concentration and equanimity. This chant names all seven in full, each in its own clause, with nothing abbreviated.

It is the first of three parallel discourses printed one after another in the book — to Mahākassapa, to Mahāmoggallāna and to Mahācunda. All three share the setting at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove and very nearly the same words, and they are chanted as a set.

**Meaning and purpose**

The exchange at the opening repays reading slowly. The Buddha asks whether the pain is subsiding rather than increasing. Kassapa answers that it is not: the feelings are severe and increasing, and their increase is what is apparent. Nothing is softened on either side, and no comfort is offered in reply to it.

What is offered instead is the list. The Buddha does not say the factors will cure anything — he says they lead to direct knowledge, to full awakening, to Nibbāna. Kassapa's reply is not a request but a confirmation: assuredly, Blessed One, they are factors of awakening. He is agreeing with something he already knows.

Then the last line, reported flatly: he rose up from that illness, and in that way the illness was abandoned. The recovery is recorded, not promised, and the whole use of the chant at sickbeds rests on that distinction. What is recited to someone ill is a description of what leads to awakening. That a recovery followed is left standing as a thing that happened.

## พระวินัยสังเขป — The Vinaya in summary

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**When it is chanted.** Chanted at a funeral, opening the Suat Jaeng service in which the Vinaya, the Suttas and the seven books of the Abhidhamma are recited in turn.

**Historical background**

Suat Jaeng is the funeral chanting in which the three divisions of the canon are each represented, one after another, by a short extract. This is the first of them and stands for the whole of the monastic discipline.

The point of such a service is not to recite the Vinaya, which would take weeks, but to have it present. Each extract is chosen to be recognisably the beginning of the thing it stands for, so that naming the opening serves for the whole.

**Meaning and purpose**

The extract opens as a catechism, question and answer. Where was the first pārājika laid down? At Vesālī. Concerning whom? Sudinna, son of Kalanda. In what matter? In the matter of his having gone back to his former wife. A pārājika is the gravest class of monastic offence, the kind that ends a monastic life rather than requiring amends for it.

Then, without any transition, the passage turns into narrative: the Buddha at Verañjā with some five hundred monks, the brahmin of that place hearing the report of him, and the long string of epithets that report carried. Both halves are openings — the first rule, and the first scene — which is what qualifies them to stand for a whole division of the canon.

What it does at a funeral is stand in for a body of text far too large to recite, and it does so by naming the very first thing in it. The chant is a gesture towards completeness rather than an argument, and the closing line makes the same point plainly: here ends the Vinaya.

**Meaning and purpose**

Then comes the method, and it is worth watching. The question is which states are wholesome, and the answer does not define anything. It describes an occasion: when a wholesome consciousness of the sense sphere has arisen, accompanied by gladness and associated with knowledge, having as its object a form or a sound or an odour or a taste or a tangible or a mental object — on that occasion there is contact, there is feeling. Only after all the conditions are laid out does it conclude: these are wholesome states.

None of this consoles, and that is close to the reason it is recited at a death. The Abhidhamma sets out what a person consists of, in categories that do not include the person. Chanting it over a body is a way of stating, in the tradition's most exact language, what has actually ended.

In a room where a body is present that is not an abstraction. The passage says what the word covers, and one of the things it covers, explicitly, is form that is past.

## พระกะถาวัตถุ — The Kathāvatthu

That this sits inside a funeral service is remarkable and worth sitting with. It is the one place in the set where the question of whether there is a person at all is put directly, and what is recited is not a consoling answer but a refutation — a text that declines, at exactly the moment one would expect it, to say the obvious thing.

## ธัมมะสังคะณีมาติกาปาโฐ — The Matika of the Dhammasaṅgaṇī

**Source (printed in the book):** อภิ.สํ. 34/1-2

**What it is.** The table of headings of the first book of the Abhidhamma — the triads under which every state is classified, recited in full.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted at funerals and memorial services, where it is known simply as the Mātikā.

**Historical background**

มาติกา means a matrix or table: the list of headings under which a treatise is organised. This is the mātikā of the first book of the Abhidhamma, whose opening lines were chanted a few pages earlier in the Suat Jaeng set.

**Meaning and purpose**

Every line is a triad, and the first is the one the whole Abhidhamma rests on: wholesome, unwholesome, indeterminate. Then states associated with pleasant feeling, with painful, with neither. And so on through the whole list, ending on states visible and impinging, invisible and impinging, and invisible and non-impinging.

The pattern almost never varies: two opposed terms, and then a third that is neither. That third is where the work is done. It is what forces the scheme to cover the whole of experience rather than only the clear cases at either end, and it is why the list can claim to be exhaustive.

That claim is what makes it a funeral chant. What is recited is a scheme complete enough that nothing whatever falls outside it. Said over a death, it amounts to a statement that the person who has died was, all the way through and at every moment, describable in these terms — and in no others.

## วิปัสสะนาภูมิปาโฐ — The Passage on the Grounds of Insight

*page 43 · chant 23 in the book · English is a working translation*  
`wipassanaphumi-patho`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The five sets that insight practice works over — aggregates, sense bases, elements, faculties and the four noble truths — named in full.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted among the general chants, following the Abhidhamma material, which it restates as the ground for practice.

**Historical background**

วิปัสสะนาภูมิ means the grounds, or the terrain, of insight. This is not a teaching about how to practise insight but a list of what insight examines — the ground it is to be worked over.

Nothing in it is new. Every one of the five sets appears elsewhere in this book, and the aggregates were set out a few pages earlier in the Abhidhamma extracts. What this chant does is gather them into one place and fix their order.

**Meaning and purpose**

The five sets are given from the coarsest analysis to the finest. The five aggregates. Then the twelve sense bases, each sense paired with its object, with mind counted as a sense and mental objects as its field. Then the eighteen elements, which are those same twelve with the consciousness that arises at each added — seeing counted alongside eye and form.

Then the twenty-two faculties, which take in not only the senses but life itself, femininity and masculinity, and the feelings; and last the four noble truths. The order is the argument. Each set divides the same experience more finely than the one before it, and the truths come last because they are what all the dividing was for.

This is why it is worth reciting a list. Insight in this tradition is not a mood or a state that arrives, but attention paid to specified things in a specified order. The chant is that specification, held in the memory where it can be reached for.

## ถวายพรพระ — Offering blessings to the Buddha

## ชะยะมังคะลัฏฐะกะคาถา — The Eight Verses of Victory and Blessing

*pages 44–45 · chant 24 in the book · English is a working translation*  
`jayamangala-atthaka-gatha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Eight verses on eight occasions when the Buddha overcame an adversary, each by a different quality, with a closing verse on reciting them.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted after the recollections at a meal offering, and at almost any ceremony of blessing; widely known by its opening word, พาหุง.

**Historical background**

Eight adversaries, one to a verse: Māra on the elephant Girimekhala; the yakkha Āḷavaka; the elephant Nāḷāgiri, crazed with drink; Aṅgulimāla with his raised sword; Ciñcā with her false accusation; Saccaka the debater; the nāga Nandopananda; and the Brahmā Baka.

Every verse closes in the same shape — the danger, the quality that answered it, and then the wish that by that power victory and blessing be the hearer's. The eight are one poem rather than eight, and are almost never chanted apart.

**Meaning and purpose**

The design is in the answers, and no two are the same. Māra was overcome by the way of giving and the rest; Āḷavaka by patience and self-taming; the maddened elephant by sprinkling the water of loving-kindness; Aṅgulimāla by a mind of psychic power; Ciñcā's slander by peaceful and gentle means; Saccaka by the lamp of wisdom; the nāga by instruction in psychic power; and Baka's wrong view by the medicine of knowledge.

Not one of the eight is overcome by force, and the adversaries are deliberately unlike each other — a temptation, a rage, an animal, a murderer, a lie, an argument, a spirit and a god with a mistaken idea. Read as a list it is a catalogue of eight kinds of trouble and eight different answers, which is why it is reached for wherever a blessing is wanted.

The ninth verse turns to the person reciting: whoever says and recalls these day by day without slackness will leave behind manifold misfortunes. The poem ends by recommending itself. That is unusual enough to notice, and it is a fair description of how the chant has actually been used — daily, by people who want the eight answers close to hand.

## ชะยะปะริตตะคาถา — The Victory Protection verses

*page 46 · chant 25 in the book · English is a working translation*  
`jayaparitta-gatha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Verses of blessing spoken over the hearer — victory at the Bodhi tree, a good dawn, and auspicious acts of body, speech and mind.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted after the verses of victory, continuing the sequence of blessing at a meal offering or a ceremony.

**Historical background**

This follows directly on from the eight verses of victory, and the change between them is a change of address. Those described what the Buddha did. These turn to the person standing in front of the reciters and say เต, yours.

The last six lines are a single triad said three times over, changing only the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha — the same refrain that closes the chant after this one, which is how the whole sequence is stitched together.

**Meaning and purpose**

The opening takes the Buddha's own victory and uses it as ground for a wish: victorious at the foot of the Bodhi tree, increasing the joy of the Sakyans — so may you too be victorious. The blessing works by likeness rather than by transfer. Nothing is said to pass from him to the hearer; the hearer is invited to stand in the same relation.

The middle is the best-known part and the most practical. A good star, a good blessing, a good dawn, a good rising, a good moment. Then, immediately: auspicious the act of body, auspicious the act of speech, auspicious the act of mind, auspicious your aspirations — and having done what is auspicious, one gains auspicious benefits. Good fortune is moved off the calendar and onto conduct. The good day is made rather than found, and that is the whole argument of the passage.

The closing triad then asks protection by the power of the Buddhas, of the Dhamma, of the Sangha. Coming where it does in a ceremony, this is the point at which attention turns from the tradition to the people in the room, and what it tells them is not that a day has been made lucky but that they will make it so themselves.

## มงคลจักรวาฬน้อย — The Lesser Universal Blessing

*pages 46–47 · chant 26 in the book · English is a working translation*  
`mongkhon-chakkrawan-noi`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** A blessing invoking every power in turn, that all illness, fear, danger and ill omen be destroyed and every good thing grow.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted at the close of a sequence of blessing, and wherever a comprehensive blessing is wanted.

**Historical background**

จักรวาฬ is the world-system, and this is the lesser of a pair — the greater blessing of the same name is longer. It works by accumulation rather than by argument: it names powers until there are no more to name, then names troubles until there are no more of those.

It closes on the same triad as the chant before it, by the power of the Buddhas, the Dhamma and the Sangha in turn, which binds the sequence of blessing into one piece.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first movement is a list of what is being invoked: all the Buddhas, the whole Dhamma, the whole Sangha, then the three jewels together, then the eighty-four thousand divisions of the Dhamma, the three baskets of the canon, and the Conqueror's disciples. The accumulation is the method. Nothing available is left uninvoked.

Then the two lists the chant exists for. What is to be destroyed: all your illnesses, fears, dangers, misfortunes, ill omens, inauspicious things. What is to grow: long life, wealth, good fortune, renown, strength, beauty, happiness — may it be so always.

Both lists are entirely worldly and the chant is not embarrassed by that. No doctrine is taught here and nothing is asked of the hearer. This is the tradition doing the thing people most often actually come to it for, on an ordinary difficult day, and doing it without pretending to be doing something more refined.

## กาละทานะสุตตะคาถา — Verses from the Kāladāna Sutta — giving at the right time

*page 47 · chant 27 in the book · English is a working translation*  
`kaladana-sutta-gatha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Verses on giving at the right time, and on how those who rejoice in a gift or help with it share its merit without lessening it.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted at ceremonies of giving, particularly where many people have had a hand in one offering.

**Historical background**

กาละทานะ is timely giving, and the title names the discourse the verses are taken from. The book prints the verses only, without the prose that would have framed them.

They are short, and their argument is almost entirely about other people — which is unusual in a text about giving, where the giver is normally the subject.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first claim concerns timing. The wise give at the right time, responsive and free from meanness; and a gift given at the right time to those who are upright becomes abundant. What makes a gift large is not its size but its moment and its recipient.

The second is the reason these verses are chanted at all. Those who rejoice in the gift, or who lend their service to it, do not thereby lessen it — they too share in the merit. Merit is explicitly not divided by being shared, and nothing is taken from the giver by others joining in.

That is a practical doctrine in a practical place. At a ceremony where one household has paid and a dozen have carried, cooked and arranged, or where many have contributed and one hands the thing over, this is the text that says nobody in the room is a spectator. It ends by making the point plainly — so let one give with an unhesitating mind.

## สังคะหะวัตถุคาถา — Verses on the grounds of kindness

*page 48 · chant 28 in the book · English is a working translation*  
`sangahavatthu-gatha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Four grounds of kindness — giving, kindly speech, helpful conduct and treating others as equals — called the linchpin that holds the world together.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in thanksgiving at occasions of communal giving; the book prints a Thai line beneath the title naming when it is used.

**Historical background**

สังคะหะวัตถุ are the grounds of kindness, or the bases of drawing people together. There are four of them, and they are among the most quoted lists in Thai Buddhism for lay life — the part of the teaching that is about living with others rather than about the mind.

Beneath the title the book prints a Thai line naming the occasion the verses belong to, which is recorded in the file with the chant.

**Meaning and purpose**

The four are named at once and without elaboration. ทานัญจะ, giving. เปยยะวัชญัจะ, kindly speech. อัตถะจะริยา, conduct that is for the good of others. And สะมานัตตะตา, treating all alike, each as is fitting — the least obvious of the four and the one that does the most work, since it means making no exception of oneself.

The image given for them is mechanical and exact: they are the grounds of kindness in the world as the linchpin is to a moving chariot. A linchpin is small and unremarkable, and without it the wheel comes off while the chariot is still going.

Then an argument that is startling for a devotional text. Were these four not to be, no mother would gain honour for her child's sake, nor a father for his. Even the bond usually treated as the most natural in the world is said to rest on them. Kindness is made structural rather than sentimental — not a decoration on human life but the thing without which none of it holds — and that is why the wise, considering them well, attain greatness.

## โมกขุปายะคาถา — Verses on the way to liberation

*pages 48–49 · chant 29 in the book · English is a working translation*  
`mokkhupaya-gatha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** A whole course of practice compressed into thirty verses — the fourfold purity, the four protections, the three characteristics and the four truths.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted among the general chants; a summary of the practice rather than a chant belonging to any one occasion.

**Historical background**

โมกขุปายะ is the way to liberation, and the chant is a manual in verse. It opens by saying what it is about to do — I shall tell the way to liberation — and then does it, in order, without digression.

That makes it unlike almost everything around it. Most of the chants in this book are lists, narratives or blessings. This one is an argument, in which each step is offered as following from the last.

**Meaning and purpose**

It begins with conduct, on the ground that nothing else stands without it: the Pātimokkha to be fulfilled, restraint of the faculties, purity of livelihood, and reflection on the requisites — the fourfold purity of virtue, to be made spotless by doing and by not doing.

Then the four protections to be developed: recollection of the Buddha, loving-kindness, the contemplation of the body's foulness, and mindfulness of death — and insight along with them. Each is then given a verse or two of its own as the chant proceeds, so the list is not merely named but worked through.

The analysis follows, and it is reasoned rather than asserted. The five aggregates arise and cease; having been thus, they are no more, so they are impermanent. Being oppressed again and again by that arising and passing, they are suffering. Not bending to one's will, and being empty and ownerless, they are not-self. The three characteristics are reached by argument, which is rare in a chanted text, and the conclusion is an image — they are like a house on fire, enough to make one seek release.

It closes with the four truths in a single verse and then an instruction with a telling word in it: knowing the meaning of even these few passages, let the wise one practise. The chant does not claim to be sufficient. It claims only to be enough to begin on, which is a modest thing for a summary of the whole path to say about itself.

## ระตะนัตตะยัปปะภาวะสิทธิคาถา — Verses on the accomplishing power of the Triple Gem

*pages 49–50 · chant 30 in the book · English is a working translation*  
`ratanattayappabhavasiddhi-gatha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Verses on the power of the Triple Gem which turn, halfway through, into a prayer for the Thai nation, its sovereign and the standing of the dispensation.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted on state and national occasions, and wherever a blessing on the country is wanted.

**Historical background**

The chant opens as many others do, with the three jewels described in turn. Then it does something no other chant in this book does: it names a country. ทัยยะเทโส, the Thai land; ทัยยะชาติ, the Thai nation; and ธัมมะขัตติโย, its righteous sovereign.

It is therefore a composition of a particular time and place. The Pali is the tradition's language but the subject is a modern kingdom, and the verses were written for a state that had taken the Triple Gem as the banner of its realm and the sanction of its law.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first third is conventional and carefully made: the Buddha as compassionate awakener of those ready to be trained; the Dhamma as the render of the world's darkness, leading out from suffering; the Sangha as a mine of merit, purified in virtue and in view. Together they are the highest refuge, and — a nice touch — fit for the ranging of the knowledge of wise examiners. The claim is that it will bear inspection.

Then the turn. The Thai land, having come to this, stands established in the happiness of peace; it has honoured and guarded the Triple Gem, made it the banner of the realm and the Dhamma's sanction for its law, and made the Sangha sovereign in the affairs of the dispensation. Every clause joins the religion to the state and says the arrangement works.

The last third is prayer rather than description: may the Thai nation shine and reach abundance and growth; long may the dispensation stand in the world; long may the sovereign live, free from illness and from fear; and for those who guard the realm, may every success be lasting. It is worth reading carefully, because it shows something a preface would only assert — the relation this tradition has actually had with the state in this country, set out in the tradition's own liturgical language.

## The Hymn to the Dhamma

*pages 53–54 · English is a working translation*  
`dhammabhigiti`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The hymn answering the recollection of the Dhamma, built on the same pattern as the hymn to the Buddha before it.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the evening service immediately after the recollection of the Dhamma's qualities.

**Historical background**

The second of the three hymns. Its opening stanza is its own; from the fifth verse it follows the first hymn word for word, changing only its object. The three are meant to be recognisably one form, and the book prints each in full rather than abbreviating the repeats.

The opening divides the Dhamma four ways — into path, fruit, scripture and release — where the morning verses divided it into three. The extra term is scripture, the teaching as it has been handed down and can be studied.

**Meaning and purpose**

The image in the opening is of holding rather than of light, though it uses both: the Dhamma holds up those who hold to it and keeps them from falling into the wretched worlds, and it is saluted as the dispeller of darkness. What it does is described before what it is.

Then the shared template returns, and its force is different when the object is a teaching rather than a person. The second of the objects of recollection; I am the Dhamma's servant, the Dhamma is my lord and master; the Dhamma destroys suffering; to the Dhamma I surrender this body and this life. Calling oneself the servant of a teaching is a stranger thing than calling oneself the servant of a teacher, and the verses do not soften it.

## The Qualities of the Sangha

*page 54 · English is a working translation*  
`phra-sangkhakhun`

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the evening service after the hymn to the Dhamma, as the third recollection.

**Historical background**

พระสังฆคุณ is the name in use, and this is the longest of the three recollections. The same words stand in the morning service inside a frame of praise; here they are given plain, with nothing added.

It is longer than the other two for a structural reason rather than an emphatic one, and the fifth line is where the extra length goes.

**Meaning and purpose**

Only after being defined is it praised — worthy of gifts, of hospitality, of offerings, of respect — and then given the phrase that explains all four: the unexcelled field of merit for the world. The Buddha and the Dhamma can be recollected by naming their qualities. The Sangha has to be said what it is first, because the word is used loosely everywhere else.

## The Hymn to the Sangha

*pages 54–55 · English is a working translation*  
`sanghabhigiti`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The last of the three hymns, answering the recollection of the Sangha and closing the central movement of the evening service.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the evening service immediately after the recollection of the Sangha's qualities.

**Historical background**

The third hymn, completing the set. Its opening stanza calls the Sangha born of the true Dhamma, of eight kinds, whose body and mind are the dwelling place of virtue and the other excellent qualities, and salutes that wholly pure company.

From the fifth verse it repeats the shared template for the third time. Anyone who has chanted the evening service through has by this point said the same fourteen lines three times over, changing one word in each.

**Meaning and purpose**

By now the template is familiar, and its familiarity is the point rather than a weakness. Read across all three hymns the effect is cumulative. The same complete devotion, in identical words, is offered three times to three different objects, with nothing ranked above anything else. A tradition that wanted to place the Buddha above the other two had an obvious opportunity here and did not take it.

Then the last prostration and the last lowering of the voice, and the request for pardon with สังเฆ. The evening service reaches its quietest point here, and what is said in that quiet is not praise at all but an apology — which is how a day, in this tradition, is properly put down.

## พุทธะมังคะละคาถา — The Auspicious Verses of the Buddha

*pages 55–56 · English is a working translation*  
`buddhamangala-gatha`

**When it is chanted.** Chanted for blessing and protection, where the arrangement of the eight directions is being invoked.

**Historical background**

**Meaning and purpose**

Then the claim that makes it a protection. These are all established here, revered by us and honoured with offerings, and by their power may every blessing be ours. The word doing the work is here. The arrangement is not being described at a distance; it is being asserted of the room the chanting is happening in.

The closing four lines step back from the circle to the Triple Gem, and end where a great deal of this book ends — with merit gained by the act of salutation, and obstacles asked to be destroyed by the power of it. It is protection by population: a space with the Buddha at its centre and eight of his disciples at its edges has, on this account, no room left for anything else.

## สีลุทเทสะปาโฐ — The Summary of Virtue

*page 56 · English is a working translation*  
`siluddesa-patho`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The Buddha's instruction to dwell accomplished in virtue, quoted in full and then taken up by the reciters as their own undertaking.

**When it is chanted.** Recited by monastics among the regular reflections, as a periodic restatement of what the training asks.

**Historical background**

สีลุทเทสะ is a summary or setting-out of virtue. The passage is short and its construction is unusual: four lines of the Buddha's words, and then the same four lines again, spoken by the assembly about itself.

Nothing is added in the second half and nothing is left out. What changes is who is speaking and what mood the verbs are in.

**Meaning and purpose**

The quoted instruction has four parts. Dwell accomplished in virtue and in the Pāṭimokkha. Dwell restrained by that restraint, accomplished in conduct and in resort — that is, in how one behaves and where one goes. And, seeing danger in the slightest fault, train in the rules of training already undertaken.

Then the turn: therefore we should train ourselves thus. The four are repeated with every verb moved out of the imperative and into the first person plural future — we shall dwell, we shall train. The instruction has become an undertaking without a word of it being altered.

That is the whole point of the passage, and the reason it is recited rather than read. An instruction heard is not the same as an undertaking made, and the gap between the two is where most training quietly goes. This text closes the gap in a single breath, and ends on a line that is neither quotation nor promise but agreement: thus indeed should we train ourselves.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted immediately before the ten training rules, as the piece that introduces them.

**Historical background**

บทขัด is an introductory verse: a short piece in metre set before a chant to say what it is and why it exists. The book prints three of them in a row on these pages, each followed by the passage it introduces.

They all end the same way, with ภะณามะ เส — let us now recite — handing the assembly over to the chant itself. The form is a hinge, and it exists because a bare list of rules arrives without any context of its own.

**Meaning and purpose**

It gives a compressed history before the rules. The Perfectly Awakened One arose in the world; he set rolling the Wheel of Dhamma that none can turn back; and to show kindness to the world he walked it on his journeys, setting the threefold true Dhamma rightly going.

Then it narrows, in two lines, to one place and one reason: coming in due course to Sāvatthī and dwelling in the Jeta Grove, and having regard for the novices' minds and for how they were to train. Only then are the ten allowed.

That narrowing is what the introduction is for. It makes the rules an answer to a situation rather than a code handed down from nowhere — somebody young needed training, and ten rules were permitted for them. The verse then steps aside and invites the assembly to recite what was allowed.

## สิกขาบท 10 — The Ten Training Rules

*page 57 · English is a working translation*  
`sikkhabot-10`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The ten training rules of a novice, from refraining from killing to refraining from accepting gold and silver.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted after its introductory verse, and recited when novices take or renew their training.

**Historical background**

These are the ten a novice undertakes. Read beside the eight kept by lay people on the observance day, also printed in this book, the relationship between the two lists becomes clear — and it is not simply that two rules have been added.

The passage is framed at both ends by the same formula: he allowed, that Blessed One, ten training rules for novices, and that novices should train in them.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first difference from the eight-precept list is a division rather than an addition. There, dancing, singing, music and unseemly shows are joined to garlands, scents and adornment in one rule. Here they are separated into two. That alone takes eight to nine, without adding anything new to abstain from.

The tenth is genuinely new, and it is the one that marks a life rather than a day: refraining from accepting gold and silver. Somebody keeping eight precepts for a night and a day still owns what they own. A novice does not handle money at all, and that single rule does more to change how a person lives than the other nine together.

The framing matters too. The list opens อะนุญญาสิ โข — he allowed — not he commanded. Throughout these pages the monastic rule is presented as a series of permissions granted in response to something that happened, and the grammar is kept even for the rules that are least negotiable.

## บทขัดนาสะนังคะ — The Introductory Verse to the Grounds of Expulsion

`bot-khat-nasananga`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The introductory verse to the grounds of expulsion, naming the incident that led the Buddha to allow a novice to be expelled.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted immediately before the ten grounds for expelling a novice.

**Historical background**

The second of the three introductory verses on these pages, and the shortest. Where the first gave a general history, this one gives a single case.

It names names, which chants rarely do: a novice called Kaṇṭaka, belonging to Upananda the Sakyan son, and the nun Kaṇṭakī. The offence is stated without euphemism and without elaboration.

**Meaning and purpose**

Five lines carry an entire case. An offence occurred. The Blessed One learnt of it from the monks themselves. And on that occasion he allowed the expulsion of a novice on ten grounds. Then the handover: let us now recite the grounds.

The phrase ญัท๎วา ตะมัตถัง — having learnt of that matter — is the hinge of all three of these introductions, and it says something about how this tradition understands its own rules. Nothing is legislated in advance. Each rule has an occasion, and the occasion is remembered alongside it.

That is why the case is chanted and not merely known. A list of ten grounds with no story attached would be a code, and codes invite lawyers. Kept with its occasion, the rule stays what it was — a response — and the reciter is reminded that something had to go wrong before anybody thought of it.

## นาสะนังคะ 10 สิกขาบท — The Ten Grounds for Expelling a Novice

*page 58 · English is a working translation*  
`nasananga-10-sikkhabot`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The ten grounds on which a novice may be expelled — the five gravest offences, dispraise of the Three Jewels, wrong view, and seducing a nun.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted after its introductory verse, among the passages on novice discipline.

**Historical background**

The list is enclosed at both ends by the same sentence — that a novice possessed of ten factors be expelled — with the ten set out between. Saying the frame twice, before and after, is how these passages mark a list as complete.

Expulsion here is นาเสตุง, a sending away. It ends a novice's standing rather than punishing them, and the book prints the milder alternative on the very next page so that the two are read together.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first five are the gravest of the training rules restated, and the grammar has changed in a way worth noticing. The rules say refraining from killing; this list says he is one who kills. It has moved from what a person abstains from to what a person is, and that shift is what makes expulsion the consequence rather than a penalty.

The next three are of a different kind altogether: he speaks in dispraise of the Buddha, of the Dhamma, of the Sangha. Speech against the three is placed at exactly the same level as killing and stealing, which tells you plainly what the community understood itself to be held together by.

The last two are wrong view, and seducing a nun — which is the offence the introductory verse has just finished describing. The list ends where its own story began, and a reciter who has chanted both pieces in order arrives at the tenth ground already knowing whose case it was.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted immediately before the five grounds for imposing a penalty on a novice.

**Historical background**

The third and last of the introductory verses, following the same shape as the one before it: an occasion, the Blessed One learning of it, and an allowance made in response.

## ทัณฑะกรรม 5 สิกขาบท — The Five Grounds for Imposing a Penalty on a Novice

*page 59 · English is a working translation*  
`thanthakam-5-sikkhabot`

**Source (printed in the book):** วิ.มหา. 4/171

**What it is.** The five grounds on which a novice may be given a penalty, all of them acts against the monks rather than breaches of virtue.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted after its introductory verse, completing the passages on novice discipline.

**Historical background**

The book prints its own reference for this list. It uses the same enclosing frame as the grounds of expulsion — the allowance stated, the five given, the allowance restated — so that the two lists can be compared line for line.

The comparison is the point of reading them together, and what it shows is that these five are not lesser versions of the ten. They are about something else entirely.

**Meaning and purpose**

All five concern the community rather than conduct in general. He exerts himself for the monks' loss of gain; for their harm; to deprive them of a dwelling. He abuses and reviles them. He sets monks at variance with monks.

Not one is a moral failing of the kind the ten training rules cover. A novice could keep all ten rules perfectly and still do every one of these five, which is exactly why they needed a separate allowance and a separate penalty.

The last of the five is the gravest thing on the list, and it is placed last deliberately: setting monks at variance with monks. Theft can be returned and abuse can be apologised for. A division worked into a community is the one injury on either list that nobody can simply undo.

## อะตีตะปัจจะเวกขะณะปาโฐ — The Reflection on the Requisites Already Used

*page 60 · English is a working translation*  
`atita-paccavekkhana-patha`

**Source (printed in the book):** นัย ม.มู. 12/17-8

**What it is.** The reflection made at the end of the day on the four requisites that were used during it without reflecting at the time.

**When it is chanted.** Recited by monastics in the evening, on the requisites used during the day.

**Historical background**

อะตีตะ means past, and that is the whole difference between this reflection and the one made at the moment of use. It covers the same four requisites in the same order, but afterwards. The book prints its own reference for it.

It exists because the reflection at the moment of use is very often not made. Rather than treat that as a failure to be corrected, the tradition wrote a second text for it and gave it a place in the evening.

**Meaning and purpose**

Every section opens with the same admission built into its grammar: อัชชะ มะยา อะปัจจะเวกขิต๎วา — whatever I have used today without reflecting. The text does not ask whether the reflection was made. It assumes it was not, and starts from there.

What follows is word for word the purpose clause of the earlier reflection, moved into the past: that was only for warding off cold, heat, the touch of gadflies, mosquitoes, wind and creeping things; that food was not for amusement or intoxication or adornment but for the endurance of this body. The reflection is applied retrospectively to acts already finished.

Why a tradition would print such a thing is the interesting part. It could have insisted the reflection be made at the time and left the matter there. Instead it wrote a second text for the commoner case and put it at the end of the day, where the day can be gone back over. It is a rule that expects to be forgotten, and makes provision for it rather than pretending otherwise.

## ธาตุปะฏิกูละปัจจะเวกขะณะปาโฐ — The Reflection on the Elements and on Repulsiveness

*page 61 · English is a working translation*  
`dhatupatikula-paccavekkhana-p61`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** A reflection that the four requisites and the person using them are alike merely elements, and that what is not repulsive becomes so on reaching the body.

**When it is chanted.** Recited by monastics among the reflections on the requisites, alongside those made at the time of use and at the end of the day.

**Historical background**

ธาตุปะฏิกูละ names the two things the reflection does. It reduces the requisite to ธาตุ, elements, and then it observes ปะฏิกูละ, repulsiveness. Both moves are applied to all four requisites in turn.

With the reflection made at the moment of use and the one made at the end of the day, this completes a set of three. One asks what a requisite is for, one goes back over a day of not asking, and this one asks what the requisite and its user actually are. The book prints the same reflection again later on, with a Thai translation beside it.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first move is applied evenly, and the evenness is what gives it force. Proceeding as its conditions proceed, this is merely elements — that is to say, this robe; and the person who makes use of it is merely elements, not a being, not a soul, empty. The user is taken apart in the same sentence as the thing used, and in exactly the same terms.

The second move is stranger and more exact than it first looks. All these robes are not in themselves repulsive; yet on reaching this foul body they become exceedingly so. The repulsiveness is not located in the object, and not quite in the body either. It arises at the contact between them.

None of this is disgust at food or clothing, which the text rules out by saying plainly that they are not repulsive in themselves. What is being undercut is the sense of there being someone to whom nice things are happening. The four requisites are the last place where a monastic's preferences can still quietly operate — which robe, which almsfood, which lodging — and this reflection goes to exactly that place.

## อุททิสสะนาธิฏฐานะคาถา — The Verses of Dedication and Resolve

*page 62 · English is a working translation*  
`uddissanadhitthana-gatha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Verses dedicating merit to a very long list of beings, then resolving on the cutting off of craving and asking that Māra find no opening.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted at the close of a period of practice or a ceremony, where merit is dedicated and a resolve made.

**Historical background**

The title names two acts rather than one. อุททิสสะนะ is dedication, the giving away of merit; อธิฏฐานะ is resolve, a determination made for oneself. The verses do the first and then the second, and the join between them is easy to hear.

The book sets the chant in two stanza groups separated by a blank line, seven lines then nine, which corresponds to that division of subject.

**Meaning and purpose**

The dedication list is remarkable for how wide it casts, and for the order it casts in. Preceptors, teachers who have helped, mother and father and kinsfolk. Then the sun, the moon, the king, and people of virtue. Then brahmās, māras and indras, the guardians of the world and the devas. Then Yama, friends, human beings, the indifferent — and the hostile.

Māra and the hostile are included by name, with nothing said to justify it and no exception carved out, and then the line that gathers them: may all beings be happy. A list that has just named one's enemies and the personification of temptation arrives at that sentence without flinching.

The resolve then turns inward and the scale changes. May I swiftly come to the cutting off of craving and clinging; may whatever base states are in my mind-stream perish always, wherever I am born, in birth after birth. And the last request is the practical one, made twice: that the māras find no opening to act against these efforts. Not that they be beaten — only that they find no way in. Ending on room to keep working, rather than on a victory, is characteristic of this whole book.

## คาถาอาราธนาธรรม — The Verses Inviting the Teaching of the Dhamma

*page 65 · English is a working translation*  
`khatha-aradhana-dhamma`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Six lines asking the Sangha to give a Dhamma talk, on the ground that today is one of the days appointed for hearing it.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted by the lay assembly before a sermon, to request it.

**Historical background**

A sermon in this tradition is not simply delivered. It is asked for, and these are the words that ask. The same shape governs the precepts, which are also requested rather than administered unbidden.

The chant is short enough to be known by heart by people who chant little else, and it is heard at almost every occasion where a monk is about to speak.

**Meaning and purpose**

It opens by establishing the day rather than the need. The fourteenth, the fifteenth and the eighth of the fortnight are the times appointed by the Buddha for hearing the true Dhamma — so the request rests on a calendar, not on a mood.

Then the particular: now this fifteenth day has come, a day marked out for it, and therefore this assembly has gathered here to hear the Dhamma. The word therefore is doing the work. The gathering is presented as the consequence of the day, not as a favour being sought.

Only then the request itself, and it asks two things at once: that the Sangha would give a teaching, and that this whole assembly, giving it their full attention, would listen. The second half is the reason the chant is worth its six lines. The people asking commit themselves in the same breath, so nobody can request a sermon and then not attend to it.

## คำถวายดอกไม้ ธูป เทียน ในวันวิสาขบูชา — The Words for Offering Flowers, Incense and Candles on Visākha Pūjā Day

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The offering made on Visākha Pūjā, the day that commemorates the Buddha's birth, awakening and final passing together.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted on Visākha Pūjā while flowers, incense and candles are offered, usually before the circumambulation.

**Historical background**

The four festival offerings are built to one plan. Each opens with the same clauses — the Blessed One gone to for refuge, our teacher, in whose Dhamma we delight — then recounts what the day commemorates, and closes by asking that the offerings taken up be accepted, for our welfare and happiness for a long time to come.

Visākha is the first and largest of the four. What it commemorates is a coincidence of dates rather than one event: the tradition holds that the Buddha was born, reached awakening, and passed to final nibbāna on the same full moon, and the chant treats all three together.

**Meaning and purpose**

The recital of the life follows the invitation of the page before, but at greater length and in shorter units — the book sets much of it a few words to a line, which is how it is chanted: slowly, with the assembly holding flowers.

What the offering asks for is stated with the same candour found throughout this book. The Blessed One is long since gone; he is known now only by his qualities and discerned only as an object belonging to the past. That concession is made explicitly, in the sentence immediately before the request.

And then the request anyway: that these offerings, taken up by us, be accepted, for our welfare and happiness for a long time to come. Nothing is claimed about where the flowers go. What the chant establishes is that they were offered knowingly, by people who had just said aloud that the one they are offered to is not there.

## สะระภัญญะคาถา สำหรับสวดในวันวิสาขบูชา — The Saraphañña Verses for Chanting on Visākha Pūjā Day

*pages 67–68 · English is a working translation*  
`sarabhanna-gatha-visakha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Verses in the saraphañña style for Visākha Pūjā, on the one who was born, awakened and quenched on the same full moon.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted on Visākha Pūjā after the offering, in the saraphañña manner rather than the ordinary reciting tone.

**Historical background**

สะระภัญญะ names a way of chanting rather than a subject. It is sung, in a melody with real intervals, and is used for verse rather than for prose. Each of the three festivals with its own set is given one.

Because the manner is fixed, the sets are recognisably a series: the same length, the same metre, and the same closing petition, with only the day's own events changed.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first verse states the coincidence the day rests on in a single line: he who on the Visākha full moon was born into his final birth, who reached full awakening, and who likewise passed to final nibbāna. Three events, one date, no comment offered on the fact.

The middle verses turn to what he was rather than what happened to him — the unsurpassed teacher, whose dwelling is the ocean of compassion and knowledge, the guide upon the path of release — and then to what the reciters have done about it: to that Buddha of great compassion we have gone for refuge.

The close is the same petition the offering made, now in verse: that the offerings, thus and thus well prepared, be accepted for our welfare and our happiness for a long time to come, for the avoiding of loss and the increase of gain. Sung rather than said, it is the part of the day most people remember.

## โอวาทะปาติโมกขาทิปาฐะ สำหรับสวดในวันมาฆบูชา — The Passage of the Exhortation Pāṭimokkha and What Follows, for Chanting on Māgha Pūjā Day

`ovada-patimokkhadi-patha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**When it is chanted.** Chanted on Māgha Pūjā after the sung verses, as the longest chant of the day.

**Historical background**

The scale of the argument is then given, and it is deliberately absurd: throughout ten thousand world-systems an uproar arose over the question, reaching as far as the dwellings of the brahmās. The verse is making a point about how hard an apparently simple question turns out to be.

Then the resolution, in a single line — that which the Refuge of the World taught, the destroyer of all evil — and the handover. Twelve years of confusion have been built up in order to make the plain list that follows land as an answer rather than as advice.

## มังคะละสุตตัง ปะฐะมัง — The Maṅgala Sutta, the First

**What it is.** The Discourse on Blessings: a deity asks what the highest blessing is, and is answered with a list running from good company to an unshaken mind.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the paritta service after its prefatory verse; among the best known of all Buddhist texts.

**Historical background**

The book prints its own reference for it, and calls it the first Maṅgala Sutta because it prints the text again later in the book.

**Meaning and purpose**

The question comes in the deity's own verse and repeats exactly the failure the prefatory verse described: many devas and human beings have pondered what blessings are, longing for well-being. Tell us, then, the highest blessing.

It starts where anybody can start — not keeping company with fools, keeping company with the wise, honouring those worthy of honour — and then moves through living in a fitting place, much learning and skill, a discipline well trained, speech well spoken, supporting mother and father, caring for wife and children, work free of confusion, giving, blameless action, restraint from strong drink, reverence, humility, contentment and gratitude. Nothing exotic appears until a long way down.

The closing verse is why it is chanted for protection: having done such things as these, undefeated everywhere, they go in safety everywhere. Safety is presented as the consequence of conduct rather than of the chanting — and the text is nonetheless chanted for safety. The tradition holds both of those at once and has never seemed troubled by it.

## ระตะนะสุตตัง ทุติยัง — The Ratana Sutta, the Second

**What it is.** The Discourse on the Jewels: seventeen verses each closing on the truth of the Buddha, the Dhamma or the Sangha, and a wish for well-being.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the paritta service after its prefatory verse; among the most used of all protective texts.

**Historical background**

The book prints its own reference, and calls this the second Ratana Sutta because the text appears again later. The prefatory verse before it has already said what the sutta is held to have done: dispelled a threefold fear of disease, non-human beings and famine.

Its construction is the plainest example in the book of how a paritta is thought to work. Each verse states something held to be true, and then asks for well-being on the strength of it having been said.

**Meaning and purpose**

The opening is an address rather than a claim, and it is the same move that opens the whole service: whatever beings are gathered here, whether of the earth or of the sky, may all of them be glad of mind and listen carefully to what is spoken. Nothing is asked of them but attention.

Then the long middle, where each verse builds to the same kind of close. Something is asserted about the Buddha, or the Dhamma, or the Sangha; the verse names it a jewel; and then, by this truth, may there be well-being. The pattern repeats until it becomes the sound of the thing rather than an argument in it.

The last verses turn from asserting to saluting, and the beings called at the start are addressed once more. The Tathagata, honoured by devas and humans, we salute: may there be well-being. It ends where it began, with the audience it summoned, having spent eighty-nine lines giving them something true to hear.

## บทขัด กะระณียะเมตตะสุตตัง — The Prefatory Verse for the Karaṇīya-metta Sutta

*page 86 · English is a working translation*  
`bot-khat-karaniyametta-suttam`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The prefatory verse to the discourse on loving-kindness, on what its practice keeps away and what it gives.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted immediately before the Karaṇīya-metta Sutta, introducing it.

**Historical background**

Like the other prefatory verses this one says why the text matters rather than what it contains, and it does so by listing results.

What it promises is unusually domestic. Not victory, not liberation, but an untroubled night.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first claim is protective in the ordinary sense: by the power of this text the yakkhas show nothing frightful. The verse is introducing a chant used where people are afraid, and it says so at once.

The second is the one worth pausing on. One who applies himself to it, night and day untiring, sleeps happily, and sleeping sees no evil thing at all. The benefit named is sleep — and the condition attached to it is continuous practice, not recitation.

That pairing sets up the sutta exactly. What follows is not a spell against spirits but a description of how to hold every living thing in mind, and the introduction quietly says that the protection and the practice are the same thing.

## กะระณียะเมตตะสุตตัง ตะติยัง — The Karaṇīya-metta Sutta, the Third

*pages 86–87 · English is a working translation*  
`karaniyametta-suttam-tatiyam`

**Source (printed in the book):** ขุ. ขุ. 25/13 ขุ.สุ. 25/353

**What it is.** The discourse on loving-kindness: what a practitioner should be like, whom to extend it to, and how to sustain it.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the paritta service after its prefatory verse, and widely used on its own as a meditation text.

**Historical background**

The book prints its own reference. It is titled the third because of its position in the Tamnan, not because there are other versions of it.

It is one of the few texts in this book that is as much used outside chanting as in it. What it sets out is a practice, in order, and the order survives being read as instructions.

**Meaning and purpose**

It does not begin with loving-kindness at all. The first five verses are about character: able, upright, easy to speak to, gentle, without conceit, contented, easily supported, with few duties and a frugal way of life, with senses calmed, not forward, not greedy among families. Only somebody already like that is considered ready for what follows.

Then the extension, and its reach is the point. Every living thing whatever, weak or strong, long or short, seen or unseen, near or far, born or seeking birth. The list is built to leave no category out, and it is followed by the image the sutta is remembered for — as a mother would protect her only child with her life.

The close moves it from feeling to posture. Standing, walking, sitting or lying down, let him resolve upon this mindfulness: this, they say here, is the divine abiding. And then, in the last verse, what it leads to — not falling into views, virtuous and endowed with vision, he comes never again to lie in a womb. A text chanted to make people feel safe ends by describing the end of rebirth.

## ขันธะปะริตตัง จะตุตถัง — The Khandha Paritta, the Fourth

*pages 87–88 · English is a working translation*  
`khandha-parittam-catuttham`

**Source (printed in the book):** วิ.จู. 7/11-12 อํ.จตุกก. 21/94-95

**What it is.** A declaration of loving-kindness towards the four royal families of snakes and towards all creatures, asking that none come to harm.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the paritta service, and traditionally used as a protection against snakes and venomous creatures.

**Historical background**

The book prints its own reference for it. The chant is addressed to snakes by family name — the Virūpakkhas, the Erāpathas, the Chabyāputtas and the Kaṇhāgotamakas — which is why it is reached for where snakes are a real danger.

Its method is worth stating plainly, because it is not what a protective charm usually does. Nothing is commanded and nothing is repelled. Loving-kindness is declared, and the safety is said to follow from the declaring.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first movement extends metta by name and then by shape. Loving-kindness for each of the four snake families; then for the footless, the two-footed, the four-footed and the many-footed. Between them those two lists cover everything that moves.

Only then the request, and it is symmetrical with what was just given: let no footless creature harm me, let no two-footed harm me. The chant asks for exactly what it has offered, in the same words and the same order, which is the whole of its argument.

It closes by widening past the bargain — let the creatures depart — and then by homage: to the Blessed One, and to the seven Perfectly Awakened Ones. Having spent itself on snakes, it ends where every other chant in this collection ends, and the protection is left resting on that rather than on the snakes having agreed to anything.

## ฉัททันตะปะริตตัง — The Chaddanta Paritta

*page 88 · English is a working translation*  
`chaddanta-parittam`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** A few verses from the story of the elephant who, pierced by a hunter's dart, sees the ochre robe and will not strike back.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the paritta service, paired with the Khandha Paritta and closed by the same colophon.

**Historical background**

The shortest of the parittas in this stretch, and the only one that is purely narrative. It takes a scene from a birth story rather than a formula, and does not explain itself.

The elephant of the story is the Great Being, the Buddha in an earlier life. The hunter has come to kill him for his tusks.

**Meaning and purpose**

The scene turns on a moment of recognition. As he touched him, thinking I shall kill him, he caught sight of the ochre robe, the banner of the seers — the hunter is wearing a monk's robe as a disguise, and the elephant sees it while being killed.

The thought that arises is given as a maxim rather than a feeling: the banner of the arahants is not to be slain by the good. What restrains the elephant is not affection for the hunter but a rule about what the robe means, held to even when the robe is being used to murder him.

That is why it is chanted for protection, and the logic is unlike the other parittas here. The others declare loving-kindness or state a truth. This one simply shows what somebody did while pierced by a dart and afflicted — and offers that as the thing worth having power in the world.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted immediately before the Mora Paritta, introducing it.

**Historical background**

It is then called พ๎รัห๎มะมันตันติ, declared to be a Brahma-spell, before the handover. That is an old and frank word for what a paritta is, and this collection does not avoid it.

## โมระปะริตตัง ปัญจะมัง — The Mora Paritta, the Fifth

*pages 88–89 · English is a working translation*  
`mora-parittam-pancamam`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The peacock's salutation to the rising and setting sun, and to the Buddhas, chanted morning and evening for a safe day and night.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the paritta service; traditionally said at dawn and at dusk, as the peacock is said to have said it.

**Historical background**

The chant is a pair of matched halves and its structure is a timetable. One half salutes the sun as it rises and asks for safety through the day; the other salutes it as it sets and asks for safety through the night.

Between them the two halves cover the whole of a day, which is what the story requires: the peacock is said to have chanted the first on waking and the second before sleeping.

**Meaning and purpose**

The sun is addressed with real warmth and no theology: here he rises, the one with vision, the sole king, golden-hued, illumining the earth. It is saluted as a benefactor rather than worshipped as a god, and then asked plainly — guarded by you this day, may we dwell through the daylight.

The petition is then handed on from the sun to those who know: those brahmins who are masters of knowledge in all things. The protection is asked for twice over, once from the light and once from the wise, and the chant does not treat these as competing.

The close is the part that carries the day into the tradition: homage to the Buddhas, homage to the awakening; homage to the liberated, homage to liberation. And then a last line that is almost domestic — having made this paritta, the peacock settles down to rest. The chant ends by describing somebody going to sleep.

## วัฏฏะกะปะริตตัง ฉัฏฐัง — The Vaṭṭaka Paritta, the Sixth

*pages 89–90 · English is a working translation*  
`vattaka-parittam-chattham`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The quail's act of truth: having neither wings that fly nor feet that walk, it states what is so, and the fire turns aside.

**When it is chanted.** Chanted in the paritta service, and traditionally used as a protection against fire.

**Historical background**

This is the clearest example in the book of a สัจจะกิริยะ, an act of truth: a formal statement of something true, made in order that its being true should have an effect.

Everything in the chant is arranged to show the mechanism working, which is why it is short. There is a helpless creature, a true statement, and a result.

**Meaning and purpose**

It begins by establishing that truth is a thing with force in the world at all: there is in the world the quality of virtue, truth, purity and compassion; by that truth I shall make an unsurpassed act of truth. The claim is made before it is used.

The truth then stated is startling for its plainness. There are wings, but they cannot fly; there are feet, but they cannot walk. The quail asserts nothing about its virtue or its destiny. It states its own helplessness accurately, and that is the whole of the act.

The result is reported in the same flat register: when my act of truth was made, the great blazing flame spared sixteen karīsas, as a flame does on reaching water. And the closing line names what the chick is said to have been practising all along — there is nothing equal to my truth: this is my perfection of truth. Not courage and not power. Accuracy.

## ท์วัตติงสาการะปาโฐ — The Passage on the Thirty-Two Parts of the Body

*pages 217–218*  
`dvattimsakara-patha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** A reflection on the body from the soles of the feet up and the crown of the hair down: bounded by skin, full of impurities, and enumerated part by part.

**When it is chanted.** Recited as a reflection on the body, commonly in the evening service and as a support for meditation on its constituent parts.

**Historical background**

This is one of the oldest and most widely used reflections in the tradition, and the Thai chanting books print it in a fixed shape: a preamble that frames the body as a bounded, impure whole, followed by the enumeration of its thirty-two parts. The parts are chanted as a bare list, each with its Thai gloss beneath.

This completes the enumeration begun in the previous paste. The list runs from the liver through the solid organs, then through the twelve liquids, and ends with the brain in the skull — the part that some older recensions omit, giving thirty-one, and that the Thai books consistently include.

The chant then returns to its opening. The same four lines that framed the body before the list are repeated after it, with เอวะมะยัง, thus is this, in place of อะยัง โข, this indeed. The reflection is therefore circular by design: the survey is stated, the evidence is given, and the survey is stated again, now with the list standing behind it.

**Meaning and purpose**

The preamble establishes the boundaries of what is to be examined before naming anything within them. This body of mine, from the soles of the feet upward, from the crown of the hair downward, bounded all round by skin. The two directions are given so that nothing is left outside the survey, and the skin is named not as a covering but as a limit — that which encloses what follows.

Only then does the judgement come: full of impurities of various kinds. The Thai keeps this in the plainest possible terms, ของไม่สะอาด, things not clean. Nothing is said about the body's ugliness in the abstract; the claim is about contents, and the list that follows is the evidence.

The enumeration itself carries no adjectives. Head hair, body hair, nails, teeth, skin, flesh, sinews, bones, marrow, kidney — each named and no more. The reflection works by inventory rather than by rhetoric, and the practitioner is left to draw the conclusion from the list itself. The register of the Thai preserves this restraint exactly: เบื้องบนแต่พื้นเท้าขึ้นมา, an older construction than modern Thai would use, and no more emphatic than the Pali it renders.

The second half of the list turns from what is solid to what is fluid. Bile, phlegm, pus, blood, sweat, fat, tears, grease, spittle, mucus, joint-fluid, urine. Twelve of the thirty-two parts are liquids, and the Thai marks each with น้ำ, water — น้ำดี, น้ำเหงื่อ, น้ำตา — so that the chanted list has an audible rhythm of its own in Thai that the Pali does not have.

The last item is set apart by its construction. Where every other part is named in a single word, the brain is given as มัตถะเก มัตถะลุงคัง, the brain in the skull, with its location stated. The Thai expands this further to เยื่อมันสมองในกะโหลกศีรษะ. It is the innermost and least accessible of the parts, and the chant ends its inventory there.

The closing recapitulation adds one word to what was said at the start: อย่างนี้, thus, in just this way. The body was described as full of impurities before the list was given; it is described so again afterwards, and the second statement rests on the first having been demonstrated part by part. Nothing new is asserted. The reflection simply asks that the same sentence be heard differently the second time.

## ภาระสุตตะคาถา — The Verses of the Discourse on the Burden

*pages 218–219*  
`bharasutta-gatha`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** The five aggregates are a burden, the person their bearer; taking it up is suffering in the world, and setting it down is happiness.

**When it is chanted.** Recited among the reflections on the five aggregates, and as a short teaching on clinging and its laying down.

**Historical background**

These verses close a short discourse on the burden, in which the burden, its bearer, its taking up and its laying down are defined in turn. The chanting books print the verses alone, without the prose that precedes them, which is why the entry opens directly with ภารา หะเว.

The Thai translation here is unusually colloquial in two places — เน้อ at the end of the first line and แหละ in the second are conversational particles rather than the literary vocabulary this register normally uses. Both are reproduced as printed. They give the opening a spoken, almost admonitory tone that the Pali does not itself carry.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first stanza states a proposition in four steps, each a bare equation. The five aggregates are a burden; the person is the bearer of that burden; taking the burden up is suffering in the world; setting the burden down is happiness. Nothing is argued. The verses simply name four things and let their arrangement do the work.

The word ปุคคะโล in the second line has occupied commentators for a long time, since the discourse elsewhere denies a person as an ultimate entity while here naming one as the burden's bearer. The Thai renders it บุคคลแหละ, with the particle throwing emphasis onto the word — the person, precisely — rather than softening it. The verses are describing how the matter appears, not conceding a self.

The second stanza describes the one who has finished. Having set down the heavy burden, he does not take up another; he has pulled out craving together with its root; he is without hunger and wholly quenched. The Thai adds พระอริยเจ้า, the noble one, naming the subject the Pali leaves implicit, and closes with ไม่มีส่วนเหลือ, with no remainder left. The sequence matters: not taking up another burden comes before the uprooting of craving, because the second is what makes the first possible.

## ติโลกะวิชะยะราชะปัตติทานะคาถา — The Verses on Dedicating Merit, of the King Victorious in the Three Worlds

*pages 220–221*  
`tilokavijaya-pattidana`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** A dedication of all merit done by body, speech and mind to every being, percipient or not, with the wish that all obtain excellent food.

**When it is chanted.** Recited as a dedication of merit, sharing the fruit of one's good actions with all beings whether they know of it or not.

**Historical background**

Pattidāna verses give away what has just been gained. Where an anumodanā rejoices in another's gift, this chant assigns the reciter's own accumulated merit outward, and does so without reserving any portion. The title attributes the verses to a king victorious in the three worlds, though no canonical source is printed alongside them here.

The Thai translation here is expansive rather than literal, a common feature of older Thai chanting books. A three-word Pali line such as ติทะเส สุคะตัง กะตัง receives a full sentence naming the Tāvatiṃsa heaven and the potency of its rewards. The page sets two pādas to a printed line and prints the Thai as a block beneath, so the two layers run in step stanza by stanza rather than pāda by pāda.

**Meaning and purpose**

The opening movement gathers everything eligible for dedication: whatever wholesome action is to be done, done by body, speech and mind, and done such that it leads to the realm of the thirty. Nothing is set aside as too small or too private. The Thai adds that the reciter has already accumulated and fulfilled these, so what follows is the disposal of something actually possessed.

The recipients are then defined as widely as the cosmology permits. Beings without perception and beings with perception — the Thai glosses the first as those existing as mere form alone — together exhaust the categories. May they all be sharers in the fruit of the merit I have made. The formula deliberately leaves no class of being outside the gift.

A practical difficulty follows, and the verses meet it directly. Merit dedicated to those who do not know of it might seem to fall idle, so the chant asks the devas to announce it to them. This is why pattidāna verses are so often chanted aloud: the giving is completed by its being made known, and where human speech cannot reach, the devas are asked to carry it.

The closing movement narrows to a single, concrete wish. All beings in the world subsist by nutriment; may they all obtain food that is agreeable. Having dedicated merit in the largest possible terms, the chant ends by asking for the plainest possible good, and the Thai adds that this is done according to the reciter's own intention, in support of their welfare and happiness.

## บทพิจารณาสังขาร — Reflection on Conditioned Phenomena (Sabbe)

*page 222*  
`sankhara-contemplation`

**Source (written for this edition):** Dhammapada 277–279, 41

**What it is.** Reflections on impermanence, unsatisfactoriness and not-self, ending with the body laid on the ground like a discarded log.

**When it is chanted.** At every morning chanting service and before sleeping.

**Historical background**

These verses gather three teachings the Buddha returned to throughout his life. The first lines are the summary of the three characteristics — that all conditioned things are impermanent, unsatisfactory, and that all things without exception are not-self.

The closing verse comes from a different occasion, when the Buddha spoke of the body's fate once consciousness has departed: it lies on the ground as useless as a discarded piece of firewood. Thai temples place these together so that the reflection moves from a general truth to the practitioner's own body.

**Meaning and purpose**

The chant walks deliberately from the abstract to the personal. It begins with all conditioned things, narrows to the certainty of death, and ends with this body, on this ground.

The middle verses are recited in the first person on purpose — my life is uncertain, my death is certain — because the teaching is not that people die but that I will.

It is chanted morning and night rather than at funerals, so that the reflection becomes ordinary rather than reserved for grief. The intention is not gloom but urgency: a life seen as short is a life less easily wasted.

## อะภิณหะปัจจะเวกขะณะ — The Subjects for Frequent Recollection

*page 223*  
`abhinha-paccavekkhana`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** Five things to be recollected constantly — ageing, sickness, death, separation from all that is dear, and ownership of one's own kamma.

**When it is chanted.** Recited as part of the daily reflections, commonly in the evening chanting and by lay and monastic alike.

**Historical background**

This is among the best known of the short reflections, given equally to lay people and to monastics, and the book gives it a Thai subtitle — ธรรมที่ควรพิจารณาเนืองๆ, the teachings that should be reflected upon frequently — which states its purpose plainly. The last line makes the instruction explicit: these are to be reflected upon in just this way, and often.

The text carries a feature rare in a chanting book: alternative forms printed in parentheses. อะนะตีโต is the masculine form and อะนะตีตา the feminine, and likewise ทายาโท and ทายาทา. A reciter says whichever agrees with themselves. Their presence in the printed page is a sign that this reflection is meant to be spoken in the first person and meant honestly, not merely read.

**Meaning and purpose**

The first five subjects proceed by a single repeated construction: I am of such a nature, and I have not gone beyond it. Ageing, sickness and death are not presented as misfortunes that may arrive but as ธรรมดา — the ordinary way of things, what is normal. The Thai word does the same work as the Pali dhamma here: it names these not as intrusions upon life but as the constitution of it.

The fifth subject shifts from the body to what surrounds it. นานาภาโว วินาภาโว — becoming otherwise, becoming separate — is rendered by the Thai as พลัดพรากจาก, to be parted from all that is loved and pleasing. The reflection does not ask the reciter to love less; it asks them to hold what they love in the knowledge that parting is already contained in the having.

The sixth subject answers the first five. Where they describe what cannot be escaped, this one names what remains one's own: kamma as possession, inheritance, origin, kin and refuge. The construction is deliberate — each is a relationship one might otherwise claim with property, family or a protector, and each is here assigned to one's own action instead. The closing lines draw the consequence: whatever deed I do, fair or foul, of that I shall be the heir.

## เขมาเขมะสะระณะทีปิกาคาถา — Verses on the Secure and Insecure Refuge

*no page number*  
`khemakhema-saranadipika`

**Source (written for this edition):** Dhammapada 188–192

**What it is.** Five verses contrasting the refuges people run to when frightened — mountains, forests, shrines — with the only refuge that actually ends suffering.

**When it is chanted.** After the Recollection Verses.

**Historical background**

During the Buddha's lifetime, many people believed that mountains, forests, sacred trees and shrines could protect them from danger. Observing this, the Buddha taught that although such places may provide temporary comfort or a sense of security, they cannot bring lasting freedom from suffering.

These verses were spoken to show that true refuge is not found in external places, but in understanding and practising the Dhamma.

**Meaning and purpose**

The verses contrast ordinary refuges with the Triple Gem — the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha.

The Buddha explains that genuine refuge is found by understanding the Four Noble Truths and following the Noble Eightfold Path, which lead beyond suffering.

These verses are traditionally chanted to reaffirm confidence in the Triple Gem and to remind practitioners that lasting safety is found through wisdom, ethical conduct and mental cultivation rather than external places or objects.

## ธาตุปะฏิกูละปัจจะเวกขะณะปาโฐ — The Reflection on the Elements and on Repulsiveness

*no page number*  
`dhatupatikula-paccavekkhana`

**Source:** *left empty — none could be given honestly.*

**What it is.** A fourfold reflection at the moment of receiving robe, almsfood, lodging and medicine: requisite and user alike are mere elements, empty of self, made foul by the body.

**When it is chanted.** Recited over the four requisites — robe, almsfood, lodging and medicinal support — at the time of receiving or using them, and included in the evening chanting of many Thai monasteries.

**Historical background**

Monastic training requires that the four requisites be received and used with reflection rather than appetite, and the chanting books preserve more than one formula for doing so. This one takes the elemental line: whatever is received is analysed into bare elements arising according to conditions, and the same analysis is turned at once upon the person receiving it, so that neither the gift nor the recipient is left standing as a solid thing.

The text is built as a single reflection repeated four times, with only the name of the requisite and the grammatical number changing — จีวร and เสนาสนะ taking plural forms, บิณฑบาต and คิลานเภสัชบริขาร taking singular. The book marks each repetition with a rubric in parentheses naming the moment at which it is used. A reader who learns the first movement has effectively learned all four.

**Meaning and purpose**

The opening line sets the frame: what lies here is only element-matter, ยะถาปัจจะยัง, proceeding as its causes and conditions proceed. Nothing is denied existence; what is denied is that the thing possesses any nature of its own beyond the conditions currently sustaining it. The Thai renders this with สักว่าธาตุตามธรรมชาติ, merely elements after the manner of nature.

The reflection then extends to the one who uses the requisite. He too is ธาตุมัตตะโก, mere elements; นิสสัตโต, not an enduring being; นิชชีโว, not a soul or person; สุญโญ, empty of the meaning of selfhood. The force of the passage lies in this symmetry. It is not that the robe is impersonal while the wearer remains a self; the same analysis is applied to both sides of the transaction, and the act of using ceases to be an encounter between a person and a possession.

The closing movement turns to repulsiveness, and does so with a precision worth noticing. The requisites are said to be อะชิคุจฉะนียานิ, not repulsive in themselves — cloth, food, shelter and medicine are not by nature foul. It is contact with this body, perpetually decaying, that renders them so. The reflection therefore locates the impurity where the training places it: not in the world, but in the body that meets the world.
